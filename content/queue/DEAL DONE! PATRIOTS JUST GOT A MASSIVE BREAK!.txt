Moses might be on his way back. A.J. Brown returned, but he’s still searching for his rhythm. And now Quenton Nelson’s name has entered the picture.

Let’s start with the break. Monday’s injury report.

Morgan Moses was listed as a limited participant because of his foot. And after the way he left the Jets game, hurt in the second quarter, into the medical tent, then the locker room, ruled out at the start of the fourth quarter, and then seen in a walking boot afterward, seeing the veteran in that limited category gives us real hope he could face the Bears.

But there’s one detail that matters a lot, and I’m not going to skip it. The Patriots only held a walkthrough on Monday. So those participation levels were estimates. Moses didn’t go through a full practice that would prove he can handle the workload of a game.

And that distinction matters even more on a short week, with so few days between the injury and kickoff. Hope is one thing. Being cleared to play is another. The next evaluations are going to separate the two.

Now, if Moses does return, here’s why it’s such a big deal. New England gets an experienced presence back on the right edge. He can help with the communication with the lineman next to him. And he can reduce the need to keep a tight end or running back in to help block on that side.

And when fewer players have to stay in to protect, Maye has more targets running routes. More options on every snap. That changes how many ways this offense can threaten a defense at the same time.

That’s where the injury report reaches straight into the passing game. How many targets can actually attack the coverage while the quarterback works through his reads? It depends partly on what happens at right tackle.

But there’s a fair counterpoint here, and it’s actually more good news. Caleb Lomu stepped in for Moses and handled the Jets really well.

According to the Patriots’ own analysis, Lomu played 59 snaps at right tackle without allowing a single pressure. Zero. And that included 16 pass protection matchups against David Bailey.

Think about this rookie’s last two weeks. Against the Raiders, he came in for Will Campbell at left tackle and helped hold Maxx Crosby without a sack. Against the Jets, he switched to the right side and didn’t allow a pressure. Two different sides of the line. Two different games. Two strong performances.

So you could ask, why rush a veteran who’s still listed as limited? And honestly, I wouldn’t make that call based on a name. The rookie has given the coaches a real alternative.

But one strong game doesn’t finish the evaluation. A defense that’s had time to prepare a plan specifically to attack him from the first snap is a different challenge. So Moses’s health and the staff’s confidence in Lomu have to drive the decision. Not reputation.

And it’s not all good news on the line. Ben Brown was listed as a non-participant because of his knee. His absence could cost the line some flexibility. He already had to move positions during the Jets game when injuries squeezed the group.

And Alijah Vera-Tucker, dealing with a hamstring issue, was also absent from the estimated report. Remember, he’s the left guard Vrabel praised for playing every single snap this season, the guy the coaches see as part of the future of the left side.

So the coaches have to think beyond the five starters. Who covers an emergency once the game is underway? Who’s the sixth lineman, the seventh? That’s why the next participation reports, and especially the game-status designations, will tell us a lot more than Monday’s early hope.

And kicker Andy Borregales was listed as absent too, but for a personal reason, with no sign of an injury. It’s worth keeping an eye on, but I’m not going to guess about it.

So taken together, the report gives Moses a possible path back, while leaving other pieces under watch. It was the kind of update I wanted after Sunday’s scare. But it still doesn’t give us the complete answer.

And whatever protection comes out of this week is going to shape how much room A.J. Brown has to start looking like himself again.

Let’s talk about Brown. He came back against the Jets, and the Patriots won 26 to 19. But his first game at Gillette after missing four games brought more frustration than celebration.

Maye targeted him 10 times. Brown made three catches for 27 yards.

When a receiver of his caliber comes back from an ankle injury, you want to believe the connection with his quarterback will light up right away. It didn’t. And now there’s a Thursday night game ahead, with less time to fix it than a normal week.

I’m not going to brush off the frustration. Ten chances is a lot to come away with only three catches. Brown let some passes get away. And he had trouble securing the Jets’ onside kick attempt at the end.

But after the game, he refused to use the ankle as an excuse. And I like that accountability. That’s the standard an offensive leader should hold himself to.

And here’s the part a lot of people missed. The film breakdown published by the team showed another side of the story. Brown created separation on some routes, including a deep one. And some of Maye’s throws missed the right spot.

So seeing Brown get open is genuinely encouraging. Watching those chances slip away still stings. But the rough result belongs to the whole connection, not just to Brown. Even though he has his own mistakes to answer for.

And there’s another reason to stay calm. The Patriots still put up 26 points while Brown finished with only 27 receiving yards. Maye completed passes to seven different players. And DeMario Douglas led the group with seven catches, 86 yards, and a touchdown.

That spread tells us the offense didn’t have to shut down while its biggest receiving name worked his way back. Brown can still draw a defense’s attention. But targets aren’t his by default. Every route has to help the play, whether it ends with the ball in his hands or opens something up for somebody else.

Now, I can hear the objection from any fan paying attention. If Maye already spread the ball around, why lean on Brown so heavily on Thursday?

Because Romeo Doubs being out changes the whole shape of this receiver room.

Doubs went on injured reserve after hurting his shoulder against the Jets. And here’s the painful part. His touchdown against the Jets had the whole stadium on its feet. And on that exact same play, the injury turned all that excitement into worry. And Mack Hollins was already on injured reserve.

So the offense needs someone who can win on the outside, draw coverage, and give Maye a chance on a contested ball when the first read is gone. Brown has the profile for that job, even if Sunday didn’t prove he can handle all of it right away.

This isn’t about throwing every pass to the most famous name. It’s about finding the routes where Brown helps Maye, without taking away what the other receivers have built.

And protection is still the practical limit. If the line loses quickly, sending Brown deep might mean keeping another target in to block. If Maye has time, the offense can pair that deep threat with shorter options underneath.

Brown was also listed as limited because of his ankle on Monday’s estimated report. So against the Bears, I want to see how much he plays, where he lines up, and whether the timing with Maye improves on throws that actually reach the window.

The talent is back on the field. Now we need to see how much of that talent this offense can actually use.

Now, that protection problem leads to a much bigger question than who can suit up on Thursday. What if the Patriots could add an established interior lineman, not just for this season, but beyond?

But Patriots Nation, this is where things get really interesting.

The Colts fired general manager Chris Ballard. And that uncertainty in Indianapolis has put a spotlight on some of their veteran players. ESPN’s Jeremy Fowler has identified Quenton Nelson as a player who could draw interest if the Colts decide to move established talent.

Now, let me be crystal clear. That’s a possibility. It’s not a report that Nelson is on the trade block. But tell me that name doesn’t make you stop and think.

Quenton Nelson has made eight Pro Bowls. He’s earned six All-Pro selections. And at 30 years old, he’s still the kind of guard who can change what an offense asks of the players around him.

And with New England’s issues on the interior, including the concerns around Mike Onwenu and Alijah Vera-Tucker, the fit is easy to see.

Think about what Nelson would mean for Drake Maye. A stronger interior pocket gives him room to step into his throws, instead of escaping pressure up the middle. Remember what Vrabel said after the Raiders game? He liked how Maye found clean pockets and stepped up into them. Nelson builds that pocket. He makes it wall-thick.

And his power in the run game could help the Patriots stay on schedule. Cory Ker running behind Quenton Nelson? Rhamondre Stevenson running behind Quenton Nelson? That’s second-and-short all day long.

This wouldn’t just be adding an emergency starter. It would be putting a proven player into the foundation of an offense built around a young quarterback.

But I’m not going to treat a trade like it’s inevitable. There are real questions.

Nelson is in the final year of his Colts contract. So an extension would have to be part of New England’s thinking. You don’t trade for an elite guard just to watch him leave in free agency.

A second-round pick, possibly with a third-rounder added, is one hypothetical price to think about. But that’s not a confirmed asking price from Indianapolis. Nobody knows the real number yet.

And if the Patriots pay that much, they need to know whether Nelson wants to stay beyond this season, and what a new deal would cost.

And here’s the hard part. Indianapolis hasn’t said it plans to trade Nelson. And if he does become available, other contenders could chase him too. The Patriots wouldn’t be the only team calling.

So here’s my take. The Patriots should make the call and find out the real price. I’d be interested in an aggressive offer if the contract picture works, because protecting Drake Maye is an investment in every single part of this offense.

But the team can’t wait on a hypothetical trade to solve Thursday’s game. It still needs enough healthy linemen so its receivers can run routes. And with Doubs out, it also needs enough receivers to run them.

And that brings us to the deal that’s actually done.

Ryan Miller joined the Patriots practice squad after Doubs was placed on injured reserve. He’s signed. But his game-day role is still up for grabs.

With Hollins out too, New England needed more receivers just to get through a week of preparation, and possibly another option for the offense on game day. And I like the move, because it answers a real shortage.

But putting a name on the practice squad doesn’t instantly bring back the routes, the touchdowns, and the trust that left with Doubs. Let’s be realistic.

Here’s who Miller is. He’s 26 years old. He’s played in 30 NFL games with six starts. According to the Patriots’ official announcement, his career totals are 17 catches, 254 yards, and four touchdowns. Those numbers describe a depth player with experience, not a hidden star waiting to be discovered.

But there’s an eye-catching play from this season. In five games with Miami, he caught three passes for 92 yards and a touchdown. And that touchdown was a 77-yarder against the 49ers, on a throw from Malik Willis.

A play like that shows he can finish an explosive chance. Three catches over that stretch show how small the sample really is.

So there’s an optimistic case. That long catch showed Miller can make something happen with limited touches. And an injured receiver room could open up snaps he’d never get in another week.

And there’s the other side. Miami released him in October, and his NFL receiving total is still modest. He could help in practice and on special teams without becoming an immediate part of the passing game.

I land somewhere in between. We’ll learn his role from whether the team elevates him, and then from how it uses him on the field. New England could still spread the work among Brown, Douglas, Kyle Williams, Efton Chism III, and Hunter Henry, the targets who already know the offense.

I understand wanting an instant replacement for Doubs. But Miller doesn’t have to carry that burden by himself. He could provide targeted help while Brown finds his rhythm and the other receivers keep producing. That’s a fairer way to judge what this signing might give the offense.

The dream: Quenton Nelson, an eight-time Pro Bowler, as a possible trade target if the Colts decide to sell.

We need the next injury report and the active list before judging this week’s protection. And we need a real signal from the Colts before treating Nelson as anything more than a possibility.
