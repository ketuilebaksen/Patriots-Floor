Let’s start with the moment that defined this game.

New England got the ball with 7 minutes and 20 seconds left, leading 23 to 16. A one-score game against a division rival. The Jets needed just one stop, one turnover, one mistake to get the ball back with a chance to tie.

And Mike Vrabel had a choice. Trust Cory Ker after his fumble against the Raiders the week before? Or go somewhere else?

He trusted him.

Ker touched the ball eight straight times. Eight in a row. He ran for 42 of the drive’s 51 yards. He burned more than 6 minutes off the clock. And he set up a field goal that pushed the lead back out.

Patriots Nation, that’s not just a good drive. That’s a statement from a coaching staff.

Because what stands out isn’t just the yards. It’s that Vrabel stuck with Ker while the Jets were hunting for one single play to get the ball back. One fumble there, and the whole game flips. And Vrabel still handed him the ball, again and again.

Ker said the instruction before he went in was simple. Protect the team. Protect the ball. And the way he put it, the ball is the team.

I love that. That’s the whole mindset in one sentence.

And remember, his fumble against Las Vegas had tested that trust. A coach can say all the right things about giving a guy another chance. Handing him the ball when one mistake could bring the opponent right back into the game is something completely different. That’s trust you can see.

And Ker answered. He averaged 5.3 yards per carry and made New York burn through its time.

Remember what Vrabel said before the game? He said there was high confidence in Ker. He said nobody ever talks about the defender who comes back into the gap and hammers the ball out, and everybody just blames the guy holding it. He said they all share in it. And then he backed up those words with the biggest drive of the game.

Now, let me be careful here, because I’m not going to oversell it. I’m not turning eight touches into a declaration that Cory Ker is the new starting running back.

TreVeyon Henderson came into this game with an ankle issue. He played only 16 snaps, and he lost a fumble in the third quarter. His health and the game situation both mattered in that fourth-quarter decision. If Henderson had been fully healthy and secure with the ball, maybe the choice goes a different way.

And Rhamondre Stevenson is still in that room. Vrabel has said he has three capable backs, and that he’ll ride the hot hand. Sunday showed that standard in action. It didn’t settle the depth chart.

But here’s what we do know. Ker has run efficiently over the last two weeks. He now has 31 carries this season at 4.5 yards a pop. That earns him more opportunities.

Still, carrying the ball isn’t the whole job of a running back. He has to handle pass protection. He has to recognize where the pressure is coming from. He has to be dependable when the offense calls a pass. And one missed block could cost Drake Maye a lot more than a strong run gains. Especially with this offensive line getting hit by injuries.

So if Ker starts showing up in a wider range of situations, on third downs, in passing formations, that’ll be a much stronger sign that the staff’s trust in him is growing.

And here’s a detail I really liked. Henderson was happy to see his teammate get that opportunity after the game. That doesn’t decide who gets the snaps. But competition in a running back room doesn’t have to be loud to be real. And when it’s healthy competition, everybody gets better.

As a fan, I liked seeing Vrabel hold Ker accountable for the earlier mistake without freezing him out because of it. That’s the balance great coaches find.

Thursday is the harder test. There’s very little time for Henderson’s ankle to recover, and very little time for the staff to build a plan for Chicago. So the question becomes this. Is Ker getting more carries because he produced? Because he was available? Or because the staff now trusts him when Maye drops back to throw too? And that same idea, earning a bigger opportunity, is playing out on the other side of the ball. And this is some of the best news of the week.

Elijah Ponder reached Week 6 with a good problem for the coaching staff. He’s producing way beyond the role anyone expected for an undrafted player.

Against the Jets, he brought down Geno Smith, and that gave him five and a half sacks this season. And the Patriots have credited him with at least half a sack in five straight games. Five straight. That consistency means more than one big afternoon.

Because think about it from the opponent’s side. When teams plan their protection, and the same defender keeps showing up in the backfield week after week, the conversation about playing time changes. Ponder isn’t a surprise anymore. He’s a problem.

And let’s remember where this pass rush started. Before the season, there were real reasons to question it. An important edge rusher left in free agency. Harold Landry got hurt. Draymond Jones arrived to take on an important role. And rookie Gabe Jacas and Ponder got a much bigger stage than anybody expected.

An injury and a departure opened up work. And Ponder grabbed it with both hands.

And it’s not just sacks. He also has seven tackles for loss, 10 quarterback hits, a forced fumble, and two fumble recoveries. Those numbers describe different jobs. Ending a play in the backfield. Disrupting a pass. Winning a possession for his team. And the value is in the combination. He’s not a one-trick player. He’s affecting the game in every way a defender can.

But Patriots Nation, this is where things get really interesting.

If Ponder keeps winning on these snaps, the staff has to ask whether the way it divides up the work still matches what the players are actually producing.

Draymond Jones has one sack so far. Now, that doesn’t sum up everything he does. And it doesn’t mean Ponder is better at every assignment. Jones can line up in different spots, face different blocking looks, and affect a play without ever showing up in the final stat line. Vrabel himself called him a real professional who knows exactly what the game plan asks of him.

But protecting the rotation because of a name or a preseason expectation would be a mistake too. Playing time should reflect what each defender can do on a given play, and what that does to the opposing offense.

And here’s where it gets even better. Look at who Ponder plays next to. Christian Barmore and Milton Williams on the inside. If offenses spend extra blockers on the interior to handle those two, Ponder may find more room off the edge. If they send help his way instead, then another defender gets a better matchup. Sometimes a pass rusher’s impact is the extra blocker it takes to keep him away. That’s how a great front works. Everybody makes everybody else better.

Now, there’s a real counterargument, and I’m not going to skip it. Six games leave a lot of season ahead.

Opponents study tendencies. They adjust their protection. They run right at an aggressive edge defender, and they force him to choose between chasing the quarterback and setting the edge against the run.

His current pace projects to 15 and a half sacks. But that’s just a projection of today’s rate. It isn’t a promise. And comparing him to the league’s famous pass rushers grabs attention, but it doesn’t replace looking at what each player is actually asked to do.

So what excites me most? It’s the opening Ponder has created. He can push this rotation toward what players are earning right now. And he gives New England an answer at a spot that looked like a concern before the season.

Against Chicago, I want to see whether that pressure holds up in a close game, and how the Bears adjust their protection to deal with him.

And that test matters even more because of the one problem this win couldn’t hide. The turnovers.

Mike Vrabel used a word nobody in Foxborough wants to hear. Embarrassment.

Speaking on WEEI, he said the team is turning the ball over at a historic rate, and he called it ridiculous.

And remember when he said this. After a 26 to 19 win over the Jets. He wasn’t explaining a loss. He wasn’t making excuses. He was pointing straight at the flaw that the win couldn’t erase. That’s a coach who isn’t satisfied just because the scoreboard looks good.

New England committed two turnovers on Sunday. Henderson lost the ball. And DeMario Douglas had one punched out. And the Jets turned those chances into points. That’s exactly how a manageable game stays alive into the final minutes. That’s why it came down to an onside kick at the end.

And look at the bigger numbers. The Patriots have 13 turnovers through six games. Seven interceptions. Six lost fumbles. Their turnover differential is minus 6.

Now here’s the part that’s actually good news, and I want to make sure you hear it. Drake Maye has gone two straight games without a turnover. Two straight. After committing seven in the first four weeks.

Think about that. The quarterback improved in exactly the area that looked most worrying early in the season. That’s real growth. That’s the biggest fix this team needed, and Maye delivered it.

But the offense as a whole didn’t fix the problem. The lost possessions just shifted to other players. And cleaning up one player’s mistakes isn’t enough if the ball keeps coming out of the offense’s hands.

I get why somebody would say this group found a way to win. It did. The defense came up with key stops. And Ker’s last drive drained the clock. That’s real.

But responding after giving the opponent a short field shrinks your margin every single time. One lost possession takes away a chance for Maye to attack. One extra possession gives the other team another shot at your defense. And with a physical Chicago team coming on Thursday, this can’t wait. On a short week, next week starts right after the game ends.

And these turnovers don’t all have the same cause. An interception can come from a bad read, from pressure, or from a deflected pass. A fumble can come from a perfectly placed hit, or from poor ball security. So Vrabel can demand one common standard. But the correction has to fit the play.

Ker got the ball again and held on to it. Henderson has to answer after his fumble and his limited work on that ankle. And Douglas has to answer for the play where the ball came loose too. Even after he recovered that onside kick to seal the win, he knows the fumble was part of his day too.

And that’s the line between accountability and panic. Pull every player who makes a mistake, and Maye runs out of options. Shrug off a fumble as bad luck, and nothing ever changes. Vrabel is walking that line perfectly. Hold them accountable. Then trust them to fix it.

So against the Bears, watch the play calls near New England’s own end zone. Watch who gets the ball in tight situations. And watch whether the opening series show better ball security through contact. Chicago’s defense gives up very few points, and handing its offense a short field makes the job that much harder.

Vrabel’s tough words matter because he follows them with visible choices. Like trusting Ker to finish the game.

And one more player becomes especially valuable when this offense needs points without giving the ball away. Hunter Henry.

Henry was targeted five times against the Jets. He caught four of them for 48 yards, and he scored the Patriots’ last touchdown.

When New York’s defense made the reads tougher, Maye had a veteran target who could finish an important play. And New England needed those points to survive the Jets’ late response.

And here’s the trend that’s really encouraging. In each of the last three weeks, Henry has topped his best receiving performance from the first three games. His role in the passing game is producing again. And that’s not a coincidence. It lines up exactly with Maye’s turnaround.

A dependable tight end gives a quarterback a window between the linebackers and the safeties. He helps on third down. And he makes a defense respect an area of the field that doesn’t require a deep throw. That matters so much when the offense can’t afford to waste drives. A reliable target keeps a possession alive. And a possession that stays alive is one less chance for a turnover to kill it.

After the win, Henry described a game of adversity and response. The Jets’ defense made things difficult. The offense answered. And New England’s defense came up with stops of its own. His contribution was part of that whole team effort.

Now, let’s keep it real. Four catches don’t make Henry the clear top receiving threat. Five targets tell the story of one Sunday. The real question is whether this recent production holds up when the coverage changes, and when Maye needs somebody on a down he absolutely can’t afford to waste.

And not every throw to a tight end solves a pass rush. The route has to reach the right space. Maye has to read the coverage. And the protection has to let the play develop.

Henry can also be asked to stay in and block, and that means he’s not immediately available as a target. And with this offensive line dealing with injuries, Morgan Moses in a walking boot, a rookie possibly starting at tackle, that’s a real choice the staff has to make. If Chicago brings pressure off the edge or clogs up the middle, the coaches have to decide where Henry helps more.

And that choice affects everybody else. When Henry runs through the middle, a defender has to respect that space, and depending on the coverage, Maye might find an option outside. When Henry stays in to block, the quarterback might get more time, but he loses an immediate target.

We don’t know which look New England will lean on Thursday. So watch Henry’s alignment, how often he releases into a route, and what Maye does when his first read is taken away.

So let’s put it all together.

The good news after this crazy Jets game is real. Three straight wins and a 4 and 2 record. Cory Ker answered his fumble with the biggest drive of the game, and earned his coach’s trust in the clutch. Elijah Ponder is turning into one of the most consistent pass rushers on this roster, with five and a half sacks and at least half a sack in five straight games. Drake Maye has gone two straight games without a turnover. And Hunter Henry is becoming a dependable weapon again when this offense needs it most.

My read after the Jets game is simple. Vrabel isn’t handing out trust based on a resume. He’s testing who can do the job when the moment demands it. He’s holding players accountable when the ball comes loose. And he’s pushing hard for a more secure offense.

This win showed that the team can respond. But it needs consistency to control games without handing chances back to the other side.
