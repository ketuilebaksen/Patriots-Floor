Let’s start with the injury updates. Because they matter a lot for protecting Maye.

Mike Vrabel told reporters that A.J. Brown and Rhamondre Stevenson looked pretty good, based on the previous practice. But then he added that he still needed to see how the next session went.

And that next session showed an important difference between the two.

Brown traveled with the team, and he practiced throughout the week. That’s real progress.

Stevenson, on the other hand, was limited on Thursday with the knee injury he suffered in the Green Bay game. On Friday, he took part in warm-ups. But he wasn’t in uniform for practice.

So I wouldn’t lump their situations together, as if both questions have already been answered. They haven’t.

With Brown, you can see the progress. He missed the last two games after an earlier knee issue flared up. Traveling and practicing gives the offense a chance to build plays with him in mind.

But the real question goes beyond whether he’s listed as active. How many routes can he run with speed, sharp cuts, and contact? Without forcing Maye to wait for separation that may never come?

Because here’s why that matters for the quarterback. If Maye drops back and has to hold the ball waiting for a receiver who can’t get open, that’s exactly when a Lions pass rush with 27 sacks gets home. A receiver who wins quickly protects the quarterback just as much as an offensive lineman does.

A contested catch on a key down can keep a drive alive. And Brown’s presence could draw coverage his way, giving other targets better matchups. That’s the real benefit. But whether the Patriots get it still depends on how he feels on Sunday.

Now, Stevenson gives the offense a different kind of stability.

He knows the protection calls. He understands what this offense asks of its running backs. And Vrabel has praised him for exactly that, his pass protection and the way he plays without the football.

He hurt his knee in the loss to the Packers. And the news that he didn’t need surgery is very encouraging. Getting back on the field this quickly matters too.

But I can’t ignore a warm-up that didn’t turn into a full practice, just because Vrabel sounded positive on Thursday.

Running straight ahead. Absorbing contact. Picking up a blitzing linebacker to protect the quarterback. Those all put very different demands on that knee. The staff needs an answer for each job, not just a blanket green light to put him on the field.

And I get the other side. A fan could say, if they’re cleared, play your best guys and stop overthinking it. I understand the urgency after giving up 45 points to Green Bay, with a strong opponent coming next.

But using either player without a plan for his workload could backfire.

If Brown only comes in for predictable situations, Detroit can adjust its coverage. And if Stevenson can’t handle a protection assignment, the risk lands directly on Drake Maye.

Managing their snaps isn’t playing scared. It’s how you turn a recovery into something the offense can actually use.

And that shortage at running back opens up an uncomfortable conversation.

TreVeyon Henderson is out with his illness. And we all hope he makes a full recovery. His health is much more important than football. And Stevenson’s knee still leaves his availability in doubt.

That could put most of the backfield work on Cory Ker and Lan Larison.

And Ker has earned some trust. Against Green Bay, he ran for 55 yards on eight carries. Remember, he’s the guy who closed out the Jets game, carrying the ball eight straight times. But he hasn’t been tested as a full-time back, especially in pass protection.

And that’s why a name from the free agent market has come up.

Bleacher Report’s Moe Moton suggested that Najee Harris would be a fit in New England.

And I like the question that suggestion forces the team to ask. But I don’t like treating an analyst’s idea as if the Patriots are already talking to him. Nobody has reported that.

Harris has been a free agent for almost a month. Moton points to his size and what he can do in short yardage. And he rushed for more than 1,000 yards in four straight seasons with Pittsburgh.

An experienced veteran might keep drives alive without making every first down depend on Maye’s arm. And the lack of depth here is obvious. One illness and one injury shifted the workload onto two backs who haven’t been tested much in that role. And the trade deadline passed without any move at running back.

But Harris wouldn’t be a ready-made answer for Detroit. As the article notes, much of his career production came from volume, not efficiency. He got a lot of yards because he got a lot of carries. And he’s coming off a serious Achilles injury with the Chargers.

And fighting for one tough yard is one thing. Learning a new offense in a few days is something completely different. Knowing the protection calls. Recognizing a blitz. Those are the things that decide whether a running back helps the quarterback or puts him in danger.

A veteran who isn’t prepared could take work from Ker, and actually offer less security on third down.

And there’s a fair case against signing him.

Stevenson might be healthy enough to play soon. Henderson could eventually return after the illness, when he’s ready. Using a roster spot and cutting into Ker’s snaps for a short-term need would be debatable.

Ker needs to be evaluated when the defense threatens the pass too. Larison needs a role this offense can actually execute. If they deliver, the argument for Harris gets weaker. If pass protection or short yardage keeps failing, the conversation changes.

So I want a serious evaluation, not a dramatic reaction to a loss. If the Patriots look at Harris, they need to define the role, check his physical condition, and figure out how long it would take him to contribute. A visit, reported talks, or a signing would change the picture.

For now, the test is inside the building. Who carries the ball? Who blocks? And who stays on the field when Detroit knows Maye has to throw?

That answer matters a lot more than a headline with a familiar name.

Now let’s get to the shocking decision. Because it came from inside the locker room.

Milton Williams decided to fix one of this defense’s biggest weaknesses with an internal penalty.

According to Williams, any Patriots defender who gets called for offsides or encroachment will pay a 1,000-dollar fine. And the money will go to the treatment staff and other team personnel.

The amount grabs your attention. But the reason matters a lot more.

ESPN’s Mike Reiss noted that New England committed seven of those penalties over the last two games, against Miami and Green Bay.

Seven. Seven chances where the defense handed the other team free yards without making them earn it.

Now, before anybody laughs at the fine, look at what it says about this locker room.

A veteran defensive lineman is telling his teammates that aggression without control doesn’t help at the line of scrimmage.

Trying to get off the ball fast is part of the job. That’s what pass rushers do. But jumping early gives the opponent a shorter down and distance. Third-and-7 becomes third-and-2. A drive that should have ended keeps going. And every play call after that changes.

Against a team that can already move the ball, that’s a costly gift.

And it connects directly to something Vrabel said this week. He said it’s okay if the other team earns it. But when you make it easy on them, when you hand it to them, those are the plays that get you beat. An offsides penalty is the definition of handing it to them.

And it connects to protecting Drake Maye too. Every time the defense gives away a free first down, the opponent keeps the ball longer. That means Maye sits on the sideline longer. And when he finally gets the ball back, he might be chasing points.

After Green Bay put up 45 points in Foxborough, focus can’t be treated like a minor detail.

But Patriots Nation, this is where things get really interesting.

The fine puts a price on an individual mistake. But the real fix has to show up in how the whole defense acts before the snap.

Who’s communicating the alignment? Are defenders reading the offense’s movement, or guessing the snap count? The rush has to start at exactly the right moment. Otherwise, the defense trades a chance to make a play for five free yards.

And remember what Vrabel said about quick snaps this week. He said when the center has his hand on the ball, the defense should probably be ready. And when an offense uses a hard count, the defense should probably wait until the ball is snapped.

That sounds simple. But after seven penalties in two games, it clearly hasn’t been.

I’m not asking for a passive defense. I want Williams and the rest of this group to turn their energy into legal pressure. With the secondary ready to cover as long as it takes.

Now, the counterargument is obvious, and it’s strong. Money doesn’t fix technique. A player could pay the fine and make the same mistake again. And without looking at every single play, we can’t blame all seven penalties on one cause.

That’s why I want a response that goes beyond punishment. Better preparation. Clean communication when Detroit changes tempo. And discipline when the pass rush tries to anticipate the snap.

If the penalties drop, maybe the fine helped drive the message home. If they don’t, the team has to fix the method, not just collect the money.

This defense still has to prove it can hold up without giving away ground before the play even starts.

And defensive discipline only goes so far if the rest of the team keeps giving away possessions and field position too.

The Lions game in Munich is going to be a brutal test of that.

New England comes in at 5 and 4, after losing to Green Bay 45 to 27 at home. The Lions are 6 and 2.

Last week’s score doesn’t decide this one. But it exposed three things that have to change. Take care of the ball. Protect the quarterback. And get coverage that responds after a rough Sunday.

Let’s start with the ball.

Maye threw three interceptions in the first half against the Packers. And Stevenson lost a fumble. That’s four turnovers. Four times the offense came off the field early, sending the defense back out in worse conditions. And it stretched the score before the team had any time to adjust.

Now remember, Maye was just named AFC Offensive Player of the Month for October. One bad game doesn’t erase that month. But an award won’t correct a bad read either.

And Maye owned it. After the game, he said he threw the ball into spots he shouldn’t have. He said he needed to see the deep safeties better, and see the defenders jumping the crossing routes. And he said the biggest thing is playing games from ahead.

Against Detroit, what matters is how he responds when his first option is covered. Especially if the score gets tight.

And Vrabel said something important about the Lions this week. He said they don’t turn the ball over on offense at all. So if the Patriots give it away again, it’ll be really hard to come back.

But it wouldn’t be fair to put all of this on Maye. The offensive line gave up three sacks to Green Bay. And the analysis points to the absences of guards Mike Onwenu and Alijah Vera-Tucker. Onwenu is out for the season. Vera-Tucker is on injured reserve. And now Ben Brown is out too, and Walter Rouse has been in the concussion protocol.

And the Lions have 27 sacks, the third-highest total in the league. Vrabel also said this week that Detroit is one of the best teams at creating unblocked pressure. That’s a warning. Not a prediction of another disaster. But a warning.

So here’s how the Patriots protect Maye. If they stay in manageable down and distance, the run game and quick passes are still available. If they give the ball away and have to chase the score, the Lions’ pass rush gets exactly the situations it wants.

That’s the practical consequence of all the questions around Brown and the backfield. Brown could force the coverage to split its attention, but only if he’s healthy enough to run his routes. A running back can make a play call harder to read, but he also has to recognize pressure and block.

And Josh McDaniels has choices. He can get the ball out faster. Or he can keep extra help in to protect. But either choice cuts down his route options. The adjustment has to work with the players who can actually go, not some ideal version of this roster.

And Vrabel said it himself this week. When a blitzer comes free, the offense has to know it, and throw hot. You don’t have a lot of choices.

And on defense, Christian Gonzalez has to answer for his last game.

The analysis credits Christian Watson and Matthew Golden with a combined 187 yards against him in the Packers game. That’s a rough Sunday. But it doesn’t explain every coverage assignment on every snap.

And an informed fan has a fair point. A cornerback depends on the scheme, the communication, and how much time the pass rush gives him.

Still, that production calls for a correction. I want to see Gonzalez contest routes better at the line. And I want to see whether the defense gives him help when Detroit tries to go deep.

And Detroit has the weapons. Jared Goff, who Vrabel said has been playing at a high level for three years. Jahmyr Gibbs, who leads the league in touches. Amon-Ra St. Brown. Sam LaPorta. Jameson Williams.

If the Patriots give away yards before the snap, and then lose in coverage, Maye will be chasing the score again. But if the defense stays disciplined, it can get him the ball back without turning every possession into a rescue mission.

And that’s how a defense protects a quarterback too. Not by blocking for him. By getting off the field, and giving him the ball with a chance to play from ahead.

So the test is whether the Patriots can break the cycle of mistakes. Watch the first few third downs. Watch the protection calls. And watch how Gonzalez responds to the Lions’ first aggressive routes. That’s where we’ll see the difference between a week of tough talk and a correction that actually holds up.
