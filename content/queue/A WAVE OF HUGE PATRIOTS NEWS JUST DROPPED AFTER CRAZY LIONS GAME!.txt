Let’s start with the biggest news. Drake Maye.

Maye left the game in Munich with an ankle injury, after taking pressure on the opening series. And Adam Schefter reported that the Patriots fear a fracture. Maye stayed behind in Germany for additional tests, including an evaluation of whether surgery is needed.

That’s serious. Really serious. But the diagnosis and the timeline still depend on those tests. Nobody knows yet exactly how long he’ll be out, and we shouldn’t pretend to know.

And it hurts to say this after what we just watched. Last season, Maye led this team to the Super Bowl. This season, he won AFC Offensive Player of the Month in October. He had five straight games with at least three total touchdowns. He’s the most important player on this roster, by far.

The good news, if there is any, is the timing. The bye week is here. That gives the team, and Maye, extra time to get medical answers before the next game against the Chargers on November 29.

But Patriots Nation, we have to be ready for the possibility that the Patriots play their next game with someone else running the offense.

So who runs the offense if Maye can’t go?

Tommy DeVito came in against Detroit. And it was tough. He struggled to sustain drives. But let’s be fair to him. The score was already forcing him into bad situations, with throws that would be hard for any backup. And coming in suddenly, in the middle of a game, is very different from preparing all week as the starter.

On Monday, Vrabel was honest about it. He said DeVito’s performance wasn’t good enough. But he also said he trusts him, and he expects DeVito to improve during the bye week.

Vrabel also said rookie quarterback Behren Morton has been progressing in the system.

So that’s the staff’s public position. Two quarterbacks in the building who know the calls. DeVito with the experience, and Morton still developing.

And that’s where the Jimmy Garoppolo idea comes in.

Garoppolo is available. He knows offensive coordinator Josh McDaniels from their years together in New England, and he worked with McDaniels again in Las Vegas. A veteran who’s used to preparing for NFL games could help the quarterback room and give the team another option if Maye’s absence stretches out.

And let’s be honest. His history in Foxborough hits an emotional nerve for a lot of Patriots fans. Jimmy G coming back home? That’s a storyline people would love.

But here’s the key point. Vrabel said on Monday that he doesn’t see a need to bring in outside help right now. He said the staff will talk it over and evaluate, but that’s where things stand.

And that changes the whole debate. There is no reunion in motion. We’re not waiting on a deal to get done. This is a question of whether a free agent would be better than two quarterbacks who already know the system.

So let’s look at both sides.

The case for sticking with DeVito: he knows this offense. If the staff believes he can improve his reads, his protection decisions, and the timing of his throws with a full week of practice, then a veteran would just take up a roster spot without guaranteeing an upgrade.

The case for Garoppolo: if the tests show a long absence, and if practice shows the room needs more help, the math changes. A quarterback with Super Bowl experience is available.

But experience has limits too. Garoppolo hasn’t played this season. And knowing the coordinator doesn’t tell us what his body can do right now.

At the end of the day, there’s one real test. Who gives this offense the best chance to function, without asking the quarterback to fix broken protection by himself?

Because a new quarterback can organize the huddle and make the protection call. But he can’t block a pass rusher who comes through free. The game plan has to help him. Limit long third downs. Give him quick throws. Help the offensive line when it needs it.

And you can’t ask a backup to do what Maye does off script. If McDaniels builds the offense around the quarterback he actually has, the team can sustain drives. If the offense keeps depending on improvisation, even a respected veteran will look worse than he really is.

So here’s what to watch, in order. First, Maye’s medical evaluation. Then, who gets the first-team reps in practice, and how the offense is built for the next game. And if there’s a visit, a workout, or a signing, that’s when we’ll know the team has changed its position on outside help. Until then, Vrabel’s words deserve to be taken seriously.

But let’s be careful here. Because public criticism doesn’t prove the locker room is split. It doesn’t mean a group has quit on Mike Vrabel. Elliss himself said he sees the effort. Just not consistently.

And speeches don’t replace execution. Detroit played with more physicality. This team didn’t play connected football. The comments and the games back that up. But claiming the locker room is divided would take evidence we just don’t have.

And let’s give credit where it’s due. The defense did come up with two interceptions, including one from Christian Gonzalez. But Detroit still found room on other possessions, and New England couldn’t keep answering.

So what does connected football look like? If one defender slips, somebody else has to arrive with the right angle. If the first hit doesn’t bring the runner down, another player has to finish the tackle. That takes communication and technique as much as energy.

Fans can demand intensity. But the coaches have to turn that intensity into assignments that show up on film.

And while the quarterback situation dominates the conversation, there was one young player who reminded us in Munich that there are smaller, very real ways to earn a spot on this roster.

Running back Lan Larison scored his first NFL touchdown in the fourth quarter against Detroit, catching a 10-yard pass from Tommy DeVito. And it was also DeVito’s first touchdown pass as a Patriot.

Now, the game was already out of reach. That play wasn’t a comeback. But on a miserable afternoon, two players trying to establish themselves made something happen together. And that moment doesn’t disappear just because the loss was ugly.

But here’s the stat that might matter even more. According to the team’s official game notes, it was Larison’s third straight game with at least two special teams tackles. And the Patriots said no player on the team had done that since Christian Elliss in 2024.

That doesn’t make Larison an offensive centerpiece. But it shows the work he’s putting in on a unit where trust, willingness to make contact, and doing your job matter every single week. A running back with only a few offensive snaps can stay active on game day because of what he does on special teams.

One late touchdown doesn’t mean he should get a big rushing workload. We still don’t know how he’d handle pass protection, read his runs, or deal with defenses preparing to face him every week. Those offensive snaps have to be earned separately.

But with Rhamondre Stevenson dealing with a knee injury and TreVeyon Henderson still working back from his health issues, running back depth deserves attention. If either one is limited after the bye, Larison could get more opportunities, depending on their health and what jobs the staff trusts him with.

So I want to see two things. Does he stay on the coverage units? And does he get offensive looks as a quick outlet for whoever is playing quarterback?

Three straight weeks of special teams contributions deserves credit. Trust grows when you do your job today, and come back and do it again next week.

And on the right side of the offensive line, a veteran gave us a different example of commitment, on an afternoon when protection had already become the biggest worry.

Morgan Moses went down early against the Lions after Rhamondre Stevenson was tackled into the back of his legs. It was a frightening sight.

But Moses came back in the second quarter. And according to the snap count, he played 76 percent of the offensive snaps. And in Pro Football Focus’s live tracking, he wasn’t charged with allowing a single pressure or a sack.

Think about that. The team had already lost its starting quarterback on the first series. Then another protector went down. And then he came back. That carries a weight that no statistic can fully capture.

Now, that return tells us plenty about Moses’s willingness. But it doesn’t tell us how his body feels now. A player can finish a hard afternoon and still need care during the bye. And one game of PFF numbers doesn’t solve every problem on the offensive line.

Rookie Caleb Lomu stepped in for Moses in the first quarter, according to the team’s official notes. If Moses is limited after the bye, Lomu may need more reps, and more communication with the guard next to him. And even if Moses is ready, the coaches still have to review where they send help along the line.

Because the pressure that brought Maye down can’t be blamed on one blocker. Play calls, how fast the ball comes out, and the other blocks all complete that picture.
