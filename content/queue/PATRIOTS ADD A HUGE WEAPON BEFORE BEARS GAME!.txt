Let’s start with that drive, because it’s the reason we’re even having this conversation.

Against the Jets, the Patriots needed to keep the ball. Leading by one score, with the Jets desperate to get it back. And Josh McDaniels called Cory Ker’s number eight straight times.

Ker delivered 42 yards and three first downs, and burned almost 4 minutes before the two-minute warning. Even when the defense knew the run was coming.

That’s why this drive means so much more than the yardage. Think about who’s on this team. A quarterback who nearly won the MVP last season. An All-Pro receiver in A.J. Brown. Those are the guys you’d expect to close out a game.

Instead, the decisive work came from Cory Ker, Efton Chism III, and Cameron Latu.

And look at where they came from. Ker and Chism both went undrafted in 2025. Latu arrived after Philadelphia released him. Three guys almost nobody was talking about at the start of the season. And when the plan lost pieces, they were ready.

That’s not luck. Reserves who are trusted to finish a game have to know their jobs and their blocking angles. That sequence took execution, not just nerve.

And here’s a fun detail. Ker cost the Patriots a future seventh-round pick in a deal with Arizona. A seventh-rounder. That’s a tiny investment for a job that turned into anything but small. Eliot Wolf’s front office strikes again.

And Latu had already made a big hit on kickoff coverage earlier in the game. So he was contributing on special teams and on offense.

Now, does that make these three permanent starters? No. Let’s not get carried away. What it shows is readiness for different jobs when injuries change the lineup. And that’s what real depth is. Doing the job the game suddenly demands.

Let’s look at the actual blocks, because this is where the magic happened.

On a 12-yard run, Latu opened the lane for Ker.

On another gain of eight, Chism helped take Minkah Fitzpatrick out of the play.

And think about Chism for a second. He caught fans’ attention in Buffalo with his receiving. Then, with Mack Hollins hurt, he picked up some of the run blocking that Hollins used to provide. A receiver who might never touch the ball can still decide whether a run leaves you in second-and-long or moves the chains. That work almost never makes the highlights. But it moved the clock.

Remember what Mike Vrabel said about this receiver room a few weeks ago? Selfless. On a pass, serve the quarterback. On a run, serve the running back. That’s exactly what Chism did.

And the unfamiliar faces didn’t stop with the skill players. They extended all the way to the offensive line.

With three starters out, Walter Rouse and Matt Prior took over the guard spots.

Rouse arrived in a late-round pick swap with Minnesota. Prior came from Arizona’s practice squad. And Prior admitted after the game that he still doesn’t know the entire playbook. Think about that. A guy playing guard in a division game who doesn’t know the whole playbook yet, and he helped close it out.

And they had to move a defensive front that knew the run was coming.

On Ker’s first carry of that drive, Rouse pulled on a power concept and helped spring a 12-yard gain. McDaniels liked it so much he went right back to it.

Ker found room behind Prior when the guard squared up Demario Davis. And later, Rouse opened another cutback lane.

Vrabel was asked about Prior and Rouse this week, and he said it was fun to watch them take advantage of the opportunity. He said they helped, and pointed straight to that huge drive at the end of the game.

But let’s stay honest. Four good minutes don’t prove this patched-up line can last a full game. The Jets needed the ball back, and they were facing a specific run sequence. Chicago has had time to study every single replacement and attack the pass protection on the interior.

One drive can’t settle the question. But calling it all luck would be lazy too. Surviving one snap is different from coordinating eight straight runs. That drive earned trust. It didn’t earn immunity from criticism.

So next, watch whether those blocks hold up on obvious passing downs. And one of those guards is about to get his chance to show us.

Walter Rouse is set to start at left guard against the Bears tonight. According to Mark Daniels, it’ll be his first NFL start, after 12 career appearances.

And Rouse sounded excited, especially with the game being in prime time. I get it. That’s a massive moment for him. Your first NFL start, on Thursday Night Football, with the whole country watching.

But as a fan, my first reaction isn’t to celebrate. My first question is, how much will Drake Maye have to change his game because of it?

On Sunday, Rouse helped finish a win. Tonight, his job starts on the very first snap.

A starting guard has to recognize movement. He has to pass defenders off with the center. And he has to keep room in the pocket for Maye to step up when he drops back. Pressure up the middle takes that space away. And if Maye has to escape early, the deep routes lose the time they need to develop.

And remember why Rouse is in there. Alijah Vera-Tucker’s hamstring injury and Ben Brown’s knee issue opened this spot. Vrabel said both are probably not available this week. And Mike Onwenu remains on injured reserve. So the entire interior of this line is trying to reorganize at the same time.

The numbers keep me from panicking, but also from applauding too early. Rouse has allowed four pressures and one sack this season, but no quarterback hits. He’s had some useful pass protection reps. But the sample as a reserve is limited.

And a defense that’s prepared to test him all night can demand very different answers than the Jets did on that last drive. Especially this Bears defense. Vrabel himself said Dennis Allen’s unit disguises well, blitzes, and gets heavy pressure from its off-the-ball players. That’s exactly the kind of pressure that tests a first-time starting guard.

So tonight will show whether that strong rushing finish was the start of a solution, or something that hid trouble in pass protection.

I’d ask McDaniels to help Rouse intelligently, without tying down the whole offense just to protect one spot. Runs that make Chicago respect the interior. Quick throws. Clean communication between the guards, the center, and the tackles. All of that could lighten the load.

But constant help has a cost. When a tight end stays in to block, Maye loses an immediate route option. And with the receiving group already this thin, every route you remove matters more. Suddenly, the offensive line problem becomes a question about how many targets can actually get into the pattern.

If Rouse holds the pocket on obvious passing downs, New England gets a lot more than a replacement for one night. It gets breathing room while the starters recover. And it gets more freedom to choose who helps in protection.

If he struggles, the coaches might need to shorten the passing plan or add a blocker.

I’m not deciding the outcome based on one closing drive or a short NFL resume. I want to see the first third-and-long. The first simulated pressure. The first time Chicago changes its rush after the snap. That’s going to tell us so much more.

And it’ll also shape how much room the Patriots have to look outside the roster for help.

But Patriots Nation, this is where things get really interesting.

A thin receiver room doesn’t mean New England has to sit quietly and wait for injured players to heal.

The Colts released Darius Slayton on October 16th. He’s 29, and he’s available to talk to any team.

And with Romeo Doubs and Mack Hollins both on injured reserve, Drake Maye could absolutely use another outside threat.

Here’s who Slayton is. He spent seven seasons with the Giants, and he put up 296 catches, 4,435 yards, and 22 touchdowns. And his average of 15 yards per catch points to a very specific job he could do for this offense.

Stretch the coverage. Pull a safety deep. And keep defenders from squeezing the short routes.

Think about how that works. A safety who has to respect a deep threat can’t just crash down on every short window. And that opens up everything underneath.

Now picture it with this receiver room. A.J. Brown draws attention on one side. If Slayton threatens deep on the other side, suddenly DeMario Douglas and Hunter Henry find more room underneath and at intermediate depth. Kyle Williams might benefit from an attack that doesn’t depend on one outside target. It gives Maye another way to stretch the field, instead of asking Brown to draw every outside look alone.

And remember, Brown is still searching for his rhythm after his rough return against the Jets. Three catches on 10 targets. The team’s film breakdown showed he was getting open, including on a deep route, but the connection isn’t there yet. Another deep threat could take some pressure off him while he finds his timing.

Now, that’s a projection. It’s not a play the Patriots are already running. And it all depends on the offensive line. Because Maye needs time for a deep route to develop. Adding speed only makes sense with a protection plan and a clearly defined role, not as a flashy answer to an injury list.

And there’s a strong counterargument. I’m not going to hide it.

Slayton struggled to adjust to the Colts offense. He dealt with a knee issue. And he left Indianapolis without playing a single regular-season game for them. So his career numbers don’t automatically describe the player who’d take the field this week. Can he still win deep? Can his knee handle those routes at game speed?

And in a short week, learning a new system, a new quarterback, and a new playbook takes a lot more than one practice. Players already here, even less experienced ones, might give McDaniels more right now. Guys like Chism, who already know where to line up.

I take that seriously. If the medical check fails, or Slayton can only handle a narrow part of the plan, a familiar name doesn’t fix the shortage. It would ask Maye to trust routes a newcomer has barely learned. And it might take practice time away from players who already know the offense.

But there’s a practical reason to explore it. Signing a free agent doesn’t cost a draft pick. That protects future resources, and it lets New England test a complementary piece without treating him like a savior. Contract cost, health, and his own interest still matter. And we don’t have any contract numbers yet.

So here’s my position. Make the call. Check the knee. Talk about the role. If those steps point to real help, move quickly. If not, work with this roster.

Two absences have changed this receiver room. And patience can’t mean standing still.

Let me be clear, though. Slayton is on the market and worth investigating. That’s it. A reliably reported Patriots contact, a visit, a physical, or a roster move would change this conversation. Until then, it’s an option, not a signing.

Meanwhile, the coaches need an answer that’s already in the building. And two tight ends could change the snap-by-snap plan, without promising a new receiver by tonight.

With injuries hitting the receivers, Hunter Henry and Eli Raridon should carry more responsibility. And Mike Vrabel talked about both of them this week. His words actually separate their roles.

On Raridon, Vrabel said he blocks better now than before. He’s gaining confidence every day. And he’s still learning how to block at this level.

On Henry, Vrabel pointed to consistency, leadership, and the thing quarterbacks value most. Dependability. He said Henry has performed and produced every year of his career.

These are different jobs. And I don’t want the coaches pretending otherwise.

Henry can give Maye a trusted option when a play doesn’t leave time for a deep route. His value is being in the right place, staying available through a read, and giving the quarterback somewhere to go under pressure. Against the Jets, he caught four of five targets for 48 yards and scored the Patriots’ last touchdown. And with Doubs and Hollins out, that reliability matters even more.

But here’s the tension. Every snap Henry spends helping a banged-up interior line is a snap where he might not get into his route right away. So the coaches have to account for the cost of every formation. Keeping the veteran in to block might be exactly right on one snap, and a waste on the next.

And that’s where Raridon opens up a new possibility. If his blocking really is improving the way Vrabel described, New England can line him up next to the line and run or pass out of the same look, without giving away the play just by the formation. That could keep Rouse from handling every left-side read on his own, and help protect Maye on certain concepts.

But the caution is obvious. A coach’s praise doesn’t prove a young player can handle a full night against Chicago’s front. He still has to show the angles, the strength, and the communication when the defense mixes pressure and coverage. And one missed assignment could undo a lot more than a good run gains.

So this is the snap battle to watch. With two tight ends on the field, New England can add weight to the run, help in protection, and still release Henry into a route. But if the defense packs the box, Maye needs targets who can push the secondary back. Without that threat, a heavy formation just invites Chicago to squeeze the field.

Snap counts, alignments, and how often each tight end runs a route will tell us a lot more than any press conference.

Eight runs proved these reserves can execute under pressure. They didn’t prove the offense can sustain it for a full game against a defense that’s been planning for them.

I respect Sunday’s answer. And I’m demanding the next one. Protect Drake Maye, without taking every passing threat off the field.
