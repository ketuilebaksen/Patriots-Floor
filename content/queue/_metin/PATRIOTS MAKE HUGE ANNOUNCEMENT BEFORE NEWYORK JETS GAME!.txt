Now let’s talk about what a strong running game could do for Drake Maye. Because that’s where this whole conversation really starts.

Young quarterbacks almost always benefit when their offense can consistently gain yards on the ground. Think about how it works. Successful runs create manageable third downs. Third-and-3 instead of third-and-9. They reduce the pressure to throw the ball on every possession. And they help the quarterback avoid those situations where the defense knows a pass is coming and pins its ears back.

We saw what happens when Maye is forced to carry everything. Through Week 3, his PFF passing grade was 36.6, the lowest among 26 qualified quarterbacks. His turnover-worthy play rate was a league-high 8.6 percent. That’s a quarterback pressing. That’s a quarterback feeling like he has to make a play every single snap.

Then over the last two weeks, everything flipped. His passing grade is 92.6, tied for the best in the NFL. His turnover-worthy play rate dropped to 1.1 percent. Against the Raiders, he went 26 of 34 for 374 yards, four touchdowns, no interceptions, and ran one in himself.

So here’s my thinking. If Maye can do that now, what does he look like when he doesn’t have to win every game with his arm? Instead of facing constant pressure to produce explosive passing plays, he’d be operating inside a more balanced system. And balance gives the coaching staff more options. It lets Josh McDaniels attack defenses in different ways, depending on the situation, the opponent, the score. That’s how you build an offense that lasts the whole season, not just one great afternoon.

The second big advantage? Explosiveness.

Not every running back can turn an ordinary handoff into a game-changing play. That’s a special skill. A truly dangerous back combines vision, acceleration, balance, and the strength to survive contact. When the offensive line creates even a small opening, that player sees the gap and hits it before defenders have time to react.

And those explosive runs do more than move the chains. They change the momentum of a game. They energize the sideline. They put the offense in scoring position without needing a long series of difficult passes. One handoff, 50 yards, first-and-goal. That’s the dream.

We know this offense can create explosive plays through the air. Maye had 10 explosive plays against a Raiders defense that had only allowed 18 total in its first four games. Ten in one game. Now imagine adding a back who can do that on the ground too. For New England, finding a player who consistently creates those big runs would be a huge step toward building a truly dynamic offense.

The third advantage might be the most underrated one. It’s how a talented running back changes defensive strategy.

Opposing teams adjust their formations based on the threats they expect. If the Patriots show they can run the ball effectively, defenses have to bring extra defenders closer to the line of scrimmage. An extra safety in the box. Linebackers creeping up.

And what does that create? Play-action. Maye fakes the handoff, the linebackers bite, and receivers are running into wide-open space behind them.

Now think about what Maye already does on deep throws. On passes of 20 yards or more against the Raiders, he went 4 of 6 for 149 yards and two touchdowns. His deep passing grade from PFF came out at 99.9. The only other quarterbacks to hit that mark this season? Jordan Love and Patrick Mahomes.

Give that quarterback a real play-action game, built on a running back defenses genuinely fear, and you’ve got a nightmare for defensive coordinators.

And here’s the part people forget. Even when a run doesn’t produce a big gain, the threat of a powerful ground attack still affects how defenders react on later downs. That’s how a great running game improves the passing offense without the running back ever touching the football.

Now, what kind of running back should New England actually target? Because “star running back” can mean a lot of different things.

A powerful runner who excels between the tackles could help win those tough short-yardage situations. Third-and-1, fourth-and-goal, the yards nobody wants to give up.

An explosive outside runner could stretch defenses horizontally and punish opponents who don’t stay disciplined in their run fits. Get the edge, and it’s gone.

And a versatile back who can catch passes out of the backfield might offer the most flexibility of all. That gives Maye a reliable outlet when his primary receivers are covered. And we know how important that is. Remember, Henderson’s 16-yard catch against the Raiders showed how valuable a back can be in the passing game.

The right choice depends on the offensive system, the players already on the roster, and the kind of production the coaching staff wants to add. There’s no one-size-fits-all answer.

That brings us to building a complementary backfield. Bringing in a star doesn’t mean Stevenson and Henderson disappear from the game plan. Not at all.

New England could use different backs for different jobs, depending on what each does best. One handles early-down carries. Another contributes on passing downs. A more powerful option becomes the guy near the goal line. That kind of rotation helps manage workloads and keeps everybody fresher through a brutal season.

And with Stevenson managing a knee and Henderson dealing with an ankle, keeping players fresh isn’t a luxury right now. It’s a necessity. Look at the schedule. The Jets this Sunday at Gillette. Then Chicago on Thursday night. Then a road trip to Miami. That’s a short week squeezed between two division games. Running backs take a beating, and a short week makes it worse.

The challenge would be making sure the new addition gets enough touches to justify the investment, while still keeping a real role for the productive guys already here. Get that balance right, and the backfield becomes a strength. Get it wrong, and you’ve got three unhappy running backs.

Of course, the offensive line remains a critical part of all of this. And Patriots Nation, this is where the timing gets really interesting.

Even an outstanding running back can’t consistently dominate when defenders reach him before he can build momentum. Blocking assignments, communication, and the ability to create space at the line of scrimmage all drive rushing production.

And right now, this line is under real pressure. Campbell got hurt on a run play, blocking for Stevenson in the second quarter, when he got rolled up. Ian Rapoport reported that Campbell is believed to have suffered a knee sprain that will likely cost him some time. Mike Onwenu and Greg Van Roten are already on injured reserve. This team is on its third starting right guard of the season.

So who’s stepping in? Caleb Lomu. The 28th overall pick in April, who the Patriots traded up to get. Before Sunday, he’d taken only 50 offensive snaps all season. Against the Raiders, he played 47 snaps at left tackle, a career high. On 29 pass-blocking snaps, he allowed one pressure and no quarterback hits. And as a team, the Patriots held Maxx Crosby to one tackle, two quarterback hits, and zero sacks.

That’s a huge performance. And now, with Sports Illustrated saying Campbell almost assuredly won’t play against the Jets, Lomu is likely set for his first professional start.

So here’s why the offensive line matters so much in this running back conversation. If the Patriots want to get the most out of a major running back acquisition, they also have to make sure the line can support that player’s strengths. Improving both areas could produce a much bigger impact than adding a star back while ignoring the weaknesses up front.

A great running back behind a struggling line is a wasted investment. A great running back behind a line that holds up? That’s a championship-level offense.

Now let’s talk about the risks, because there are real ones, and Patriots fans can’t ignore them.

The financial and roster implications deserve serious attention. Established running backs bring experience and immediate production. But their contracts and acquisition costs have to make sense for the team’s long-term plans.

A trade could require draft capital. And this is a roster still building around a young quarterback. Every pick you spend on a running back is a pick you can’t spend on an offensive lineman or a cornerback. And we’ve seen this season how fast injuries pile up at both of those spots.

A free agent signing could involve guaranteed money and future salary commitments. Money that could also be used to extend your own young core down the road.

And running backs face huge physical demands. Durability and workload management are serious concerns at this position. The Patriots would need to look carefully at a player’s recent performance, injury history, running style, and expected role before deciding whether a major investment is justified. Paying big money for a back who breaks down in November is one of the worst mistakes a team can make.

Here’s another factor that doesn’t get talked about enough. How a stronger ground game helps the Patriots control the tempo of games.

When a team can run effectively, it sustains longer drives, keeps its own defense off the field, and limits the opponent’s opportunities. That’s especially valuable in close games, when every possession matters.

And think about this defense right now. Four cornerbacks were out heading into the Raiders game. The secondary is thin. Every long drive by the offense means fewer chances for opposing quarterbacks to test those backups. A running game that eats clock is, in a real way, a defensive strategy too.

A reliable running back also gives the offense more options in the fourth quarter. When New England needs to protect a lead. When it needs to convert a crucial third-and-short. Those contributions don’t always make the highlight reel, but over a full season, they make a massive difference. Games are won in those quiet moments.

Still, Patriots fans should remember something important. One major addition can’t solve every offensive problem.

New England still needs continued quarterback development. Dependable receiving options. Solid protection. And consistent coaching. A star running back could make this offense better, but the team would have to use him intelligently and avoid becoming predictable.

The best offenses run when defenses expect the run, and throw when opponents prepare for the pass. Wait, let me say that better. The best offenses can run when you know it’s coming, and still beat you. And they can throw when you’re ready for it, and still beat you. That uncertainty is what makes an offense almost impossible to defend. Especially when the quarterback and the running back can both create problems for opposing coordinators.

And Mike Vrabel’s message to the locker room this week fits perfectly here. He told his team that none of Sunday’s success will matter against the Jets. Every week starts at zero. That’s the mindset. No matter who’s in the backfield, the work starts over every Wednesday.
