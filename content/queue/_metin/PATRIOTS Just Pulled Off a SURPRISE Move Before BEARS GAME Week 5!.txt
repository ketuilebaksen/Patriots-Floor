Let’s start with Pop Douglas, because he’s coming off one of the most complete games of his career.

Seven catches. 86 yards. A touchdown. In the 26 to 19 win over the Jets. And then, at the very end, he recovered the onside kick that shut down New York’s last chance.

I love that combination. He produced all game long, and then he showed up on the one play where a single mistake could have handed the ball right back to the opponent. Two very different moments. And in both of them, he did exactly what his team needed.

And the need for that just changed fast.

Romeo Doubs went on injured reserve after hurting his shoulder against the Jets. Mack Hollins was already out with a calf injury.

And think about what those two meant to this offense. Doubs led the team in receiving yards before he got hurt, with 261. Hollins had 209, and he brought a physical presence to his routes and his blocks.

So let’s be clear. Douglas can’t just take over every route those two ran. That’s not how football works. But Maye still needs dependable places to throw the ball. And that’s exactly why Sunday’s performance matters so much heading into Chicago.

There’s one detail that keeps sticking with me. Douglas’s 9-yard touchdown catch helped make it 17 to 3 right before halftime. Those weren’t empty numbers in a blowout. New England had a chance to turn a drive into points, and he came through.

But he also fumbled while trying to gain extra yards after a long catch. So his game made a big difference, and it also left him with a mistake to clean up. And remember, Mike Vrabel called the turnovers an embarrassment after that game. Douglas knows he has to protect the football.

Here’s why Douglas matters so much right now. On third down, when the space gets tight, trust between a quarterback and a receiver buys time. Maye sees the window and lets it go before the pressure arrives. If Douglas keeps getting open quickly, and takes better care of the ball, he can keep this offense moving, even with fewer receivers available.

And I can already hear some fans saying, “Okay, but A.J. Brown is back. Shouldn’t the bigger burden fall on him?”

I expect plenty from Brown too. But his return against the Jets brought three catches for 27 yards on 10 targets. Chances slipped away. And the timing with Maye is still coming back after that ankle injury kept him out.

So throwing every ball his way just because of his name would trade one problem for another.

Here’s how it should work. Brown can pull coverage toward him. Douglas can work in the space that attention creates. And Hunter Henry gives Maye another way out. That combination can work, without pretending Douglas has suddenly become a traditional number one receiver.

And there’s another fair point. Don’t get carried away by seven catches. Chicago doesn’t have to give Douglas the same looks the Jets did. The Bears can close down the space inside. They can challenge the quick routes. And they can force the offense to win farther downfield.

If that happens, Douglas faces a completely different test. I want to see whether he stays on the same page with Maye when the first window closes. And whether the other targets can keep the defense from loading up on him.

Sunday gives me hope. Smaller windows will tell us how much of that hope holds up.

And those windows get a lot more dangerous when the quarterback can’t count on a clean pocket.

Up front, Walter Rouse and Matt Prior are set to start at guard against the Bears. They helped finish the game against the Jets, on that eight-run drive with Cory Ker that drained the clock. But this time, they have to answer the call from the very first drive.

And if Maye has to cut his reads short to escape pressure up the middle, even the best route concept can’t give this offense everything it was designed to give.

Look at the injury list on this line. Alijah Vera-Tucker is out with a hamstring injury. Ben Brown is out with a knee injury. Mike Onwenu and Greg Van Roten are on injured reserve. That’s four interior linemen unavailable.

And that explains the surprise move. New England looked outside its own roster.

Veteran offensive lineman Jamaree Salyer visited the Patriots on Tuesday.

And that visit shows the team knows it has to check its depth before another injury leaves it with even fewer choices.

Here’s who Salyer is. He’s 26 years old. He spent four seasons with the Chargers. And he started 43 regular-season and playoff games combined. That’s real starting experience.

He was a backup over the last two seasons. But experience at more than one spot matters a lot when a team can barely hold on to an extra option for game day. And then he spent time on injured reserve with Miami before being released in August.

So New England would still have to evaluate where he stands physically, and how much of the system he could pick up. Let me be clear. His visit is a way to examine an option. It’s not a fix for the offensive line on Thursday night. But it tells you this front office isn’t sitting still.

Now, there’s a real case for trusting Rouse and Prior. Against the Jets, they helped New England run the ball and drain the clock when the defense knew the run was coming. I love that toughness.

But a full game of pass protection demands communication that one closing drive never tested.

A defensive tackle can attack the space between the guard and the center. A blitz can force those linemen to pass off a rusher in a hurry. And if Maye loses room to step up in the pocket, Brown may have to cut a route short, and Douglas may catch the ball with defenders already all over him.

Josh McDaniels can lean on quick throws, the running game, and a tight end helping in protection. But constant help takes away passing options. If Henry stays in to block, he’s not immediately available over the middle, which is exactly where the missing receivers already hurt the most.

So I want to watch Rouse and Prior on an obvious passing down. How does the line react to movement before the snap? Can Maye get through his reads without having to run for his life?

Salyer is someone to evaluate for future depth. In Chicago, the first answer has to come from the starters Vrabel has already named.

But Patriots Nation, this is where things get really interesting.

Drake Maye is back in the MVP conversation, right as the protection in front of him has gotten less stable.

Three straight wins, over the Bills, the Raiders, and the Jets. Ten touchdown passes and just one interception in that stretch.

That’s one heck of an answer. After a rough start to the season, I’m proud to see a New England quarterback pulling this team forward. But I also feel the worry every Patriots fan knows. Are we asking him to win battles that the rest of the roster should be helping him fight?

Sports Illustrated tracked Maye’s rise in the MVP odds up to plus-1400 after Week 6. That move tells us the market is taking his season seriously again. It doesn’t measure every throw, and it doesn’t guarantee him any award. To me, it measures a change in perception.

Ten touchdowns in three games, plus a 4 and 2 record, have changed the way people see this quarterback. Remember, through Week 3, his PFF passing grade was the lowest among 26 qualified quarterbacks. Now he’s in the MVP conversation. That’s a massive turnaround.

The question on the field is whether that production can keep going when the players around him are less settled.

And there’s one number that caught my attention. Maye’s completion rate over that three-game stretch averaged 64.1 percent. That’s below the standard he’s shown before. Even so, he created touchdowns and avoided the most costly mistakes while the team adjusted to injuries.

That percentage alone can’t tell us why it dipped. But put it next to the touchdowns and the wins, and you see two things. The quality of the quarterback. And how little margin for error this roster is leaving him.

And look at what’s been happening around him. Against the Jets, Caleb Lomu replaced Morgan Moses at right tackle and gave the coaching staff breathing room. Rouse and Prior helped inside. Anybody who says the replacements have shown they can compete has a point. Now they have to repeat it against an opponent that’s prepared to attack their communication.

Moses is listed as questionable with a foot injury. His health might decide where Lomu is needed, before the coaches even think about another change.

And there’s some good news. Brown is back. And TreVeyon Henderson is available. The talent around Maye could look a lot better as those two get their rhythm back.

But Doubs, Hollins, and the missing guards don’t reappear just because two talented players returned. On every play call, the offense has to decide how many targets can release into routes, and who has to stay back to block.

Brown’s three catches on 10 targets call for work on that connection. They don’t mean the partnership should be dropped. And they don’t mean Brown can solve every absence by himself.

So here’s how I read the MVP talk. Maye belongs on the radar, because he lifted this team when the season threatened to slide early. But turning every game into another survival act by the quarterback isn’t a lasting plan. An MVP candidate can cover up a depth problem for a few Sundays. But when a defense changes its approach, the blocks and the routes have to answer alongside him.

Thursday night in prime time gives us another look at that whole structure.

Now let’s get to the toughest decision this week. Caleb Lomu.

Mike Vrabel said Lomu has to be ready to play more than one position. But he did not announce the rookie as a starting guard. Rouse and Prior will start.

And that comment points to a tough choice. Moving the young player to cover one hole could leave another spot exposed, including the spot where he’s already helped this team.

Remember, Lomu had to step in at tackle when Moses left the Jets game. And he was outstanding. 59 snaps at right tackle without allowing a single pressure. If Moses can’t play, or if his foot gives him trouble again during the game, somebody has to protect that edge. Put Lomu inside, and moving him back out means reshuffling the line in the middle of a game.

That’s why this guard discussion can’t be reduced to putting the five most talented names on the field and hoping it works. The coaches have to think about the sixth man. The next substitution. And what happens to communication when two spots change at once.

Vrabel indicated he’d stick with the group that finished Sunday. And I see the logic. Rouse and Prior earned a chance to start after helping win that game. And Lomu stays available to cover a need on the edge, with Moses questionable. That flexibility matters, even if the rookie isn’t in the starting lineup. One more injury could change the entire calculation.

But the other side has a strong argument too. If Lomu is playing well and the interior is hurting, why wait to try him at guard?

Here’s the catch. Moving from tackle to guard changes the angles and the exchanges with the center. One communication mistake inside gets to Maye fast. What Lomu has shown at tackle doesn’t automatically prove he’s mastered guard.

The Patriots can prepare alternatives and still decide that keeping Rouse and Prior together gives them the best chance to start the game organized. I want to judge that call by what happens on the field, not by where Lomu was picked in the draft.

And there’s a reason New England can even picture this kind of flexibility. Evan Lazar has pointed to the team’s emphasis on movement and athletic ability in its offensive linemen, over raw strength alone. Lomu fits that perfectly. He’s played left tackle, right tackle, and now he’s preparing for guard.

But now that idea faces a real situation. Somebody has to recognize pressure, move his feet, and keep Maye upright when the original plan breaks down. Athletic traits create options. Execution under pressure tells us which options the team can actually use.

So the next signs will be easy to spot. Who’s active. Where Lomu lines up if he comes in. And what happens to the protection after the first forced change.

I want New England to come out of this week understanding its choices better, without making Drake Maye pay for the lesson.

So let’s put it all together.

The surprise move: Jamaree Salyer, a 26-year-old veteran with 43 starts, visiting Foxborough to give this battered offensive line another option.

The receiver who has to step up: DeMario Douglas, coming off seven catches, 86 yards, a touchdown, and a game-sealing onside kick recovery, with Doubs and Hollins on injured reserve.

The line puzzle: Walter Rouse and Matt Prior starting at guard, Moses questionable, and Caleb Lomu ready to play wherever he’s needed.

And the quarterback carrying it all: Drake Maye, with 10 touchdowns and one interception over three straight wins, and his MVP odds climbing to plus-1400.

I believe in what Maye and those backups did to win three straight. And that’s exactly why I refuse to accept a miracle on every down as the plan against Chicago. The standard has to belong to everybody. Targets getting open. Guards communicating. And a clear plan if the line has to change again.

We still don’t know how much rhythm A.J. Brown has, or how long this battered protection can hold up. The first third downs, and any adjustment involving Lomu, will give us real answers.
