Let’s start with Vrabel’s decision on Caleb Lomu.

Here’s where things stand. Lomu is still behind Will Campbell and Morgan Moses on the tackle depth chart. Start there.

But look at what this kid has done. New England’s rookies have played the second-most special teams snaps in the entire league. And Lomu, the first-round pick, has twice stepped in for extended stretches as an injury replacement.

He’s played left tackle. He’s played right tackle. And he’s lined up as the extra blocker in heavy sets, filling that jumbo tight end role.

That’s a ton of work for a player who was supposed to develop slowly.

And his flexibility is a huge deal. One absence on the line doesn’t force the staff to rebuild the entire unit. And on a team that’s used 14 different offensive line combinations, with four guards out, that’s priceless.

I like what that says about his preparation. When a starter goes down, the big worry is whether the quarterback can still drop back and throw before a breakdown kills the drive.

And in the two games when Lomu saw substantial offensive action, he allowed just one pressure. One. Across 160 offensive snaps, according to the report, he’s shown he can anchor against power and recover when a defender gets the first advantage.

Remember the Raiders game? He came in for Campbell at left tackle and helped hold Maxx Crosby without a sack. And against the Jets, he played 59 snaps at right tackle without allowing a single pressure, including 16 one-on-one matchups with David Bailey.

That’s the thing about tackle. You won’t win every rep on first contact. Sometimes the job is surviving the next move. And Lomu keeps surviving.

But here’s why Vrabel isn’t rushing it. And honestly, I think it’s the right call.

There’s a huge gap between respecting that start and declaring the position solved. The sample is small. Being the extra blocker in a heavy set doesn’t demand the same reads as a full series of pass protection on obvious passing downs. And strong stretches on both sides don’t tell us how Lomu will handle opponents who study his tendencies all week long.

That’s the objection I take seriously if someone says New England has a ready-made starter who can replace either veteran. What we’ve seen gives me a lot more confidence in the depth. It doesn’t automatically change the pecking order.

And Mike Vrabel chose his words very carefully. He reminded everyone that Lomu is 21 years old. He’s new to this league. And he’s learning one of the hardest positions in football.

But Vrabel also said Lomu has improved. And I’m listening closely to that part. He’s acknowledging the adjustment, without overlooking the progress.

Remember what Vrabel said about the rookie class a couple of weeks ago? They’re 21- and 22-year-old kids. You have to invest in them and can’t lose sight of them, even if they’re not playing 80 percent of the snaps. And they’re more coachable than some of the veterans. This is exactly what that looks like.

So for Foxborough, the gain is a third tackle the team doesn’t have to hide when a game changes in a hurry. Campbell and Moses keep their starting roles when they’re available. But if another absence comes, the staff has seen Lomu respond, even if the matchup still calls for some help.

And that choice gets tougher when the offense has to throw a lot. So next time, I care less about Lomu’s raw snap count and more about the kind of snaps he gets.

Will they leave him alone on third-and-long? Can he hold up when a defender threatens the outside, then cuts back inside? And in those heavy sets, will his presence actually open a running lane, or will it tip off the defense about what’s coming before the snap?

Those answers will show whether his current flexibility can turn into a lasting responsibility. The early impression is encouraging. And that’s exactly why it deserves a real test instead of an early coronation.

That’s the bombshell, Patriots Nation. In a league where teams hand jobs to first-round picks the second they look good, Vrabel is making Lomu earn it. Patiently. On purpose.

Now, on the other side of the ball, a rookie made a much louder statement. Gabe Jacas.

The rookie edge rusher generated five quarterback pressures on Thursday night against Chicago. That might be the best performance of his young career so far.

On one rush, he drove straight through left tackle Ozzy Trapilo with the bull rush that’s always been part of his game. That’s power with a purpose. Push the blocker back, and take away the space the quarterback needs to breathe.

Now, five pressures don’t make anyone the defense’s top pass rusher overnight. Favorable matchups, well-designed calls, or help from teammates can all play a part.

But his progress over seven weeks points in one direction. Jacas looks more and more comfortable using the strength that got him drafted.

And weight-room strength only matters on Sunday if you place your hands right, keep your balance, and stay in the play when the tackle fights back. In Chicago, the pressure showed up in the results, not just in how imposing the rookie looked.

And think about where this edge group was before the season. A lot of us had real concerns. It looked like a weak spot. Harold Landry was hurt. A key edge rusher had left in free agency.

Now? It has more ways to attack an offense’s protection. Elijah Ponder, the undrafted second-year guy, has five and a half sacks and at least half a sack in five straight games. Draymond Jones brings the veteran savvy Vrabel loves. And now Jacas is starting to break out.

Jacas doesn’t need to carry the whole pass rush. If he demands attention when he’s on the field, the offense has less freedom to slide its protection to the same side every time. And a defender across from him might get a cleaner one-on-one, depending on the call and how the offensive line responds.

But the question that decides how big this leap really is stays open. How many of those wins can he repeat when an opponent adjusts specifically to stop his first surge of power?

The next clue will be his workload on likely passing downs. More snaps there would show the coaches’ trust goes beyond excitement over one night.

I also want to see his discipline against the run. If he only comes in to chase the quarterback, that limits his value on the edge.

This performance strengthens his case for more snaps, without forcing Vrabel to pull someone else out of the rotation. The best outcome is pressure coming from several places at once.

But Patriots Nation, this is where things get really interesting.

DeForest Buckner is in the trade conversation ahead of the November 10th deadline.

Tom Pelissero reported strong interest from other teams, and said no Colts player is expected to be off-limits if Indianapolis decides to sell. And Albert Breer named Buckner as a possible target for New England.

Now, those are two different signals. One describes interest the Colts have received. The other points to a possible fit for the Patriots. Neither one describes an actual offer from Foxborough. Let’s be clear about that.

But the change in Indianapolis is real. A 2 and 4 start, followed by the firing of general manager Chris Ballard. The franchise has to decide whether its veterans can help it fight back, or whether to turn part of the roster into assets for the future. And we’ve already seen Quenton Nelson and Jonathan Taylor’s names come up from that same situation.

The Colts could look at their next few games and decide to keep Buckner. Other teams could drive up the price. And New England has its own needs before giving up a pick for a player nearing the end of his contract.

But Buckner is producing right now. In six games, he’s recorded 26 tackles, four sacks, four tackles for loss, and eight quarterback hits.

And that combination of sacks and hits matters. An interior defender who keeps getting near the passer can speed up throws even without finishing with a sack. That changes route timing, and it forces the offensive line to worry about the middle of the pocket.

New England already has Christian Barmore and Milton Williams on the inside. The idea would be adding another threat there. One that changes who gets the best one-on-one matchup.

Picture it. Barmore, Williams, and Buckner inside. Ponder and Jacas on the edges. In some looks, the defense might create pressure without sending an extra rusher at all. In others, a deeper rotation could save energy for the key downs.

And that might matter most when an opponent is trailing and has to throw over and over. If the Patriots can create that situation, it’s a real tactical possibility. Not a promise that three big names will all work together on every snap. The staff would still have to assign roles and account for the run.

Now, the best objection comes straight from New England’s own roster. If Jacas is developing on the edge, and Barmore and Williams already bring strength inside, why spend an asset there while Drake Maye still needs protection?

I take that question seriously. Remember, this offensive line is missing four guards. Every pick spent on the defensive line is a pick not spent on protecting the quarterback. And there are offensive line options out there, from Quenton Nelson to Andrew Wylie.

A Buckner trade would only make sense if the price, the financial cost, and the plan for his contract left room to address those other needs.

The story floated a mid-round pick as one possible price. But that wasn’t a reported offer. If Indianapolis asks for more, the math changes. And it also changes if the staff believes its current interior group already gives the scheme what it needs.

So here’s where I land. I’m interested, with conditions.

Better interior pressure could shorten plays for a secondary that’s been banged up, with Carlton Davis missing three straight games and Marcus Jones being pushed outside. It could help when the offense sputters. But it won’t fix Maye’s protection, and it won’t erase the mistakes that cost New England the game in Chicago.

First, watch whether the Colts start listening to offers after their upcoming games. Until they decide, New England can’t know if there’s a real deal to chase. Then watch for a specific report about contact or a price involving the Patriots.

And that matters even more when we look at what this quarterback is giving the team.

Drake Maye threw for 321 yards in the 30 to 24 loss in Chicago. That brought him to 1,784 passing yards on the season. And after Thursday’s game, it put him in a temporary spot at number two in the league, just 10 yards behind Matthew Stafford’s 1,794.

After a shaky start, that’s exciting. But “temporary” matters. Maye played before most quarterbacks in Week 7, so the rankings could change once the week wraps up.

What matters more than his spot on the leaderboard is the shape of his season.

In the first three games, Maye completed 51 of 80 passes for 585 yards, with one touchdown, six interceptions, and a 58.6 passer rating.

Over the next four games? He completed 90 of 134 passes for 1,199 yards.

Patriots Nation, that’s a massive swing. The first stretch wasn’t a final verdict. And the second isn’t definitive proof of an elite quarterback. But the passing game started producing far more yards, and Maye topped 300 yards twice in that span.

And that production was there in Chicago, even though the final score hurt.

That response fires me up. This quarterback doesn’t look trapped in his early-season form. When the passing game gains yards consistently, defenses have to respect more of the field, and New England has more ways to sustain a drive.

But progress is more than passing yards. One explosive completion can hide a protection breakdown on the play before it. And a 321-yard game can still end in a loss. This roster has to turn Maye’s ability into drives that finish, instead of asking his arm to cover every mistake.

And there’s a fair counterpoint. Six interceptions in the first three weeks don’t disappear just because the yardage went up. Consistency means knowing when to attack and when to take the smaller play. It means holding up against defenses that take away the biggest routes.

The jump from 585 yards in the first three games to 1,199 in the next four shows real growth. It doesn’t explain every decision Maye made, or the quality of each opponent. I want to see him protect the ball and keep the offense moving when his first options are covered.

And remember what Maye said after the Bears game. He owned that interception in the end zone. He said he let his team down. That’s the mentality that turns growth into consistency.

So that brings us back to the support around him. Lomu gives the line more security. A Buckner trade, if it happened, could strengthen the defense, so a big day from the quarterback wouldn’t have to cover every mistake on the other side.

Now, that doesn’t mean a backup tackle or a possible trade caused Maye’s turnaround. Building around him takes answers in more than one place.

The MVP conversation can come if this production keeps going, and the wins follow. But today, I’d rather ask a different question. Can New England protect his momentum and turn it into victories?

If the protection gives him enough time. If he stays aggressive without repeating the early string of mistakes. And if the team finishes drives. Then his temporary ranking and his passing yards won’t be the biggest news anymore. What will matter is whether that improvement shows up again when a defense already knows what it has to stop.

So let’s put it all together.

Vrabel’s decision: keeping Caleb Lomu as the third tackle behind Campbell and Moses, even after he allowed just one pressure across 160 offensive snaps. Patience, by design.

The breakout: Gabe Jacas, with five pressures against Chicago, and a bull rush that drove a left tackle right into the quarterback.

The trade talk: DeForest Buckner, with strong league-wide interest and Albert Breer naming the Patriots as a possible fit. But no offer, and no reported contact.

And the quarterback: Drake Maye, up to 1,784 passing yards and temporarily second in the NFL, after a massive turnaround from his first three games.

I’m encouraged. But the standard stays high. The rookies have already made real contributions, and Maye’s response is something Foxborough can appreciate, without pretending the loss didn’t sting.

What’s still open is how much this team should invest in help while that growth continues. So watch the Colts’ decision before the trade deadline. And before then, watch the next long-yardage downs. They’ll tell us how much the protection and the pass rush can really hold up.
