Let’s start with TreVeyon Henderson. Because I feel it every time Drake Maye has to pull a play out of thin air.

As a rookie, Henderson topped 1,000 total yards and scored 10 touchdowns, even while the team brought him along slowly. So the expectations for year two were high.

But that burst still hasn’t turned into a reliable presence from one series to the next. His 3.9 yards per carry tells part of the story, not the whole debate.

And that inconsistency gets to me. Because the acceleration is still there. Henderson can change a defender’s angle in one step. Once he gets to the edge, the defense has almost no time to close.

And here’s a number that should slow down anyone calling him a failure. Bleacher Report’s Gary Davenport ranked Henderson second among running backs in their second season. Behind only Ashton Jeanty, who was drafted more than 30 spots ahead of him.

So the talent isn’t the question. The question is something a ranking can’t measure. Picking up a pass rusher on third down.

For a back playing next to Drake Maye, that’s the biggest concern. If Henderson can’t be trusted in pass protection, the coaching staff has a reason to limit him in certain situations. And when a defense threatens a blitz, one missed assignment can put a defender right on the quarterback.

Remember what Mike Vrabel said about Rhamondre Stevenson? Before he even met him, what stood out on tape was what kind of pass protector Stevenson was. The way he played without the football. That’s the bar Vrabel sets for his running backs.

Now, Stevenson has had his own ball security issues. So I understand the fans asking why Henderson doesn’t get more chances. But you can’t decide a snap battle by only comparing what each player does wrong.

Stevenson brings experience with the adjustments before the snap. Henderson brings the speed to break a defense wide open. Josh McDaniels has to decide which risk he can live with on each play call. And Henderson has to make that decision easier.

And there’s another side to this. Injuries have forced New England to piece together its offensive line, with four guards out at one point. It would be unfair to judge a runner without looking at the space the play gives him. But it would also be too convenient to blame every short run on the blocking. When the lane is there, Henderson has to read it and take the yards.

So over the next few games, I want to see a real sequence of opportunities for him. Including downs when a pass is on the table. Does he hold up in protection? Does he earn enough trust to stay on the field? If he does, the run game becomes a real weapon for this offense.

That’s the kind of patience I can get behind in Foxborough. Time, paired with a standard. Not an endless excuse. What he did as a rookie earns him a fair chance to answer. And how he protects the quarterback will decide how much that chance grows.

And while the Patriots try to get more out of their own roster, an outside possibility has started to stir up the receiver room.

That’s the surprise move. And the name is Josh Downs.

According to Jordan Schultz, teams that need wide receivers have been calling the Indianapolis Colts about a possible trade for the 25-year-old. And one report put the Patriots among six interested teams.

That puts New England squarely in the public conversation. Now, let me be clear. It doesn’t mean there’s been an offer. It doesn’t mean there’s an agreed price, or serious negotiations. But I’d absolutely pay attention. The trade deadline is November 10th, and injuries have put real pressure on this receiver group.

And here’s the detail that makes Patriots fans lean forward. His connection to Drake Maye.

They played together at North Carolina in 2022. And that season, Downs caught 94 passes for 1,029 yards and 11 touchdowns. With Maye throwing him the ball.

Now, I’m not saying two guys know each other and everything magically clicks in the NFL. But Maye found this target again and again for an entire season. For an offense trying to get its rhythm back, that familiarity with route timing, and with how Downs adjusts to the ball, could shorten the learning curve in a big way.

Smash that like button if you want to see Maye and Downs reunited in Foxborough.

Think about what Downs could give this offense. An answer in the short and intermediate game. A well-timed completion there means Maye doesn’t have to wait for a long route to develop behind a patched-together offensive line.

And on third down, a target who gets open quickly can change which plays McDaniels feels comfortable calling. Remember, against the Bears, Maye said he had to find answers to get the ball out faster, so his linemen didn’t have to hold their blocks so long. Downs is exactly that kind of answer.

But I have a question before I fully buy in. What role would Downs actually have here?

New England already has DeMario Douglas working inside. If Downs just duplicates that role without improving the result, giving up a draft pick looks a lot less appealing.

But if the Patriots can move those receivers around, pair their routes together, and take some pressure off an injured group, then the math changes.

And there’s a cost that the excitement tends to skip over. The Colts can take calls without accepting a deal that works for New England. A high draft pick could address another need, like the offensive line. College chemistry makes the fit interesting, but it’s not a blank check.

And a fan against the trade would say, give the young players already here a real look first. I take that seriously. A trade only makes sense if the price matches Downs’s role now, and beyond this season.

So the next signs to watch are a real conversation between the teams, a reported asking price, or a move by the Colts that changes their willingness to deal. Until then, I see an opportunity.

And that question about the talent already in the building brings us to a receiver whose playing time has grown, even if his production hasn’t caught up yet.

Kyle Williams.

Williams is on the field more often now, because this receiver room has lost options. Romeo Doubs is on injured reserve with a shoulder injury. Mack Hollins is out with a calf injury. So the opening is right there.

But the production? Six catches, 147 yards, and one touchdown through seven weeks.

For a third-round pick known for his speed, that gap between snaps and catches hurts. I wish he were already forcing defenses to adjust every week. He isn’t there yet.

And the expectations were real. Williams reached the end zone in all three preseason games. That explains why people were excited. But it doesn’t guarantee separation once the real games start.

And remember his 34-yard touchdown against the Raiders. That’s what his speed can do. It forces a secondary to respect the deep ball.

But here’s the problem. If the Patriots only ask him to run straight up the sideline, they turn a rare strength into a predictable assignment. Defenses figure that out fast.

There might be a better path, through the middle of the field. According to the Pro Football Focus numbers in the report, Williams caught all three of his targets between the numbers for 67 yards.

Now, that’s a small sample. It’s not proof he’s mastered working inside. But it gives the coaches something specific to test. Short and intermediate crossing routes. The ball arriving in stride. Room to accelerate after the catch.

And Williams put in work this offseason on his lower-body strength. He’s talked publicly about handling contact and press coverage better. So let’s test that work on the snaps that actually demand it.

The counterpoint is the less flattering detail. On two targets where he ran routes breaking back toward the line of scrimmage, he didn’t make a catch, and one of those passes was intercepted.

Now, two targets don’t define a career. Does that route fit the way he gets open? Was the ball out on time? Without those answers, I won’t put it all on the receiver. But I also won’t ask Maye to keep forcing a combination that isn’t working yet.

So here’s what I’d do. Start with a crossing route that lets Williams catch the ball on the move. Then change his release point on a similar play call. The defense would have to decide whether to chase his speed inside, or save help for the deep ball. Either way, somebody gets open.

Williams doesn’t have to become the number one target overnight. The offense just needs to find out whether he can threaten more than the sideline. And once the Patriots know that, the conversation about Josh Downs gets a lot sharper. They’d know exactly what they already have.

Meanwhile, two other players have already shown that this offense doesn’t have to wait for a trade to find production.

Look at this number from the Bears game. Drake Maye threw for 321 yards and two touchdowns. And the wide receivers accounted for just 87 of those yards, and neither of the touchdowns.

That contrast bothers me more than any target list. Maye moved the offense. But his receivers gave him very little help.

Rhamondre Stevenson helped with five catches for 93 yards and a touchdown, including that 48-yard score before halftime.

But for the question driving this video, the tight ends gave us the biggest answer.

But Patriots Nation, this is where things get really interesting.

Hunter Henry and Eli Raridon are already giving the Patriots two different answers to the same problem.

Henry caught all seven passes thrown his way against the Bears. He gained 71 yards and moved the chains five times. On a third-and-7 during the opening drive of the second half, he kept the possession alive.

And that drive ended with a 30-yard touchdown to Raridon, who beat his defender through the middle of the field. The rookie finished with three catches, 56 yards, and a touchdown on five targets.

I’m not talking about a projection here. Those plays kept New England in position to win, even in a loss.

And this didn’t just start in Chicago. Look at the trend.

Hunter Henry had seven catches for 71 yards and no touchdowns over the first three weeks. Over the next four? 19 catches for 225 yards and two touchdowns.

Eli Raridon missed Week 3 with an injury. He went from two catches for 32 yards in the first three weeks to 10 catches for 136 yards over the next four.

So the offense found a steadier point of support just as the receivers started getting hurt. That’s not a coincidence. That’s the coaching staff adjusting.

Vrabel said this about Henry. A consistent presence. A veteran leader. He’s produced every year of his career, and quarterbacks like guys who are dependable. And about Raridon, Vrabel said he’s blocking better than before, gaining confidence every day, and still learning how to block at this level.

Henry knows the spots where Maye looks for a quick answer. Raridon adds size and a threat over the middle. And according to SumerSports, Raridon’s 2.13 yards per route run ranked fourth among qualifying tight ends in the cited sample. That efficiency tells you these aren’t just emergency throws. He’s actually getting open.

But the rookie has a smaller sample. And defenses can adjust their coverage if the offense leans on him too much.

And the strongest objection is simple. If Henry and Raridon are working this well, why are we even talking about Josh Downs or Kyle Williams?

Because a defense can squeeze the middle of the field when the wide receivers aren’t hurting it on the outside. Two productive tight ends give the Patriots more formations and more routes. But they don’t replace the threats that stretch the field.

Their value grows when the rest of the offense forces a defense to choose what to protect. And that’s why Henderson improving in protection, Williams finding a real role, and any possible trade addition all connect to the same question. How does this offense spread the load?

And Miami gives us a really useful test.

In the numbers cited for this week, the Dolphins were allowing the fifth-most catches and the fourth-most receiving yards per game to tight ends. That’s a favorable matchup for Henry and Raridon.

But I want to watch more than their individual stat lines. Does Henry keep converting the important downs? Does Raridon get routes when the defense expects a throw over the middle? And if that coverage tightens, can Maye find someone outside of the congestion?

The answer will tell us whether this tight end duo is becoming the foundation of a more complete offense, or whether it’s carrying a load that should be shared.

So let’s put it all together.

The surprise move: the Patriots reportedly among six teams interested in Josh Downs, Drake Maye’s former college teammate, who caught 94 passes for 1,029 yards and 11 touchdowns with him at North Carolina. No offer, no price yet. But a real opportunity.

The running back question: TreVeyon Henderson, ranked second among second-year backs, but still needing to earn trust in pass protection.

The young receiver: Kyle Williams, with six catches for 147 yards, and a possible new role working through the middle of the field.

And the answer that’s already here: Hunter Henry and Eli Raridon, combining for 29 catches over the last four weeks, with a great matchup against a Miami defense that struggles against tight ends.

I like the answer Henry and Raridon gave us on the field. But New England’s standard demands help for Drake Maye from more than one position. Patience with Henderson has to turn into trust in pass protection. Williams’s speed has to find useful routes. And any price for Downs has to make sense next to those options.

What’s still open is whether this offense can string together good drives when Miami adjusts its coverage. So watch the first few third downs on November 1st, and who stays on the field for them.
