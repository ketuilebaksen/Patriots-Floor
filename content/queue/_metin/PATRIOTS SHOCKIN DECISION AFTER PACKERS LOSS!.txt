And all of this comes right before a massive game against the Detroit Lions in Munich, Germany.

Let’s start with the deadline itself. Because it tells us a lot.

For weeks, the Patriots were linked to big names. A prediction market had them as the favorite to land Jonathan Taylor, jumping from 5 percent to 34 percent. AtoZ Sports reported their interest in Derrick Henry. They were reportedly one of four teams in the wide receiver market, with Josh Downs, Drake Maye’s former college teammate, on the table. Albert Breer floated a possible reunion with K’Lavon Chaisson. And names like Quenton Nelson and Andrew Wylie were tied to the offensive line.

And in the end? Nothing.

Now, we don’t know why. Maybe a deal couldn’t be reached. Maybe the asking prices were too high. Maybe the front office simply has confidence in the roster it has. We don’t have that answer, and I’m not going to pretend we do.

But the visible result is clear. The coaches have to build this plan with the players already in the building.

And honestly, I can see both sides. On one hand, this team just lost by 18 points and is short on weapons. On the other hand, Taylor would have cost a first-round pick and 21 million dollars next season. Downs would have cost a third or fourth. And this front office already gave up a 2028 first for A.J. Brown. Protecting the future has value.

Now let’s get to the first big question. Who protects Drake Maye on the right side?

An Athlon Sports analysis published after the Green Bay loss made a bold argument. Start rookie Caleb Lomu at right tackle over Morgan Moses for the rest of the season.

Now, let me be clear. That’s a columnist’s proposal. It’s not an announcement from Mike Vrabel. But it goes straight to the question this offense can’t avoid. Which lineup gives Maye enough time to play, without turning every dropback into an escape drill?

Let’s look at the numbers.

Morgan Moses is 35 years old, in his 13th NFL season. According to the article, his PFF overall grade of 62.2 ranks 49th among tackles. He’s allowed 14 pressures across 489 offensive snaps.

Now, that one number can’t explain everything happening up front. And Moses brings a lot. Game awareness. Communication. Veteran know-how. But experience doesn’t automatically solve a matchup against faster pass rushers. That value has to come with the ability to hold up once the defender moves.

And then there’s Caleb Lomu. The Patriots’ first-round pick in 2026, drafted to grow alongside Will Campbell on this offensive line. Unlike Campbell, Lomu didn’t start from day one.

But in the snaps he has played, the same analysis cites a 74.9 pass-blocking grade, and just four pressures allowed.

And we’ve seen what he can do. Against the Raiders, he came in for Campbell at left tackle and helped hold Maxx Crosby without a sack. Against the Jets, he played 59 snaps at right tackle without allowing a single pressure. Across 160 offensive snaps over those stretches, he allowed just one pressure.

That’s encouraging. Really encouraging.

Smash that like button if you want to see Caleb Lomu starting at right tackle in Munich.

But here’s where I want to be fair. Putting four pressures next to 14 and declaring the competition over ignores the difference in workload. Moses has played a lot more snaps. And the rookie hasn’t played a long run of games as a full-time starter. Opponents haven’t had weeks of film to study him and attack his weaknesses.

So start with the assignments. How much tight end or running back help would each tackle need against Detroit? How many practice reps can really test the line’s communication? If the Lions disguise pressure on the right side, the test is recognizing movement before the snap, not just winning a one-on-one block.

If Lomu wins the job, he gets experience right where the Patriots see his future. If Moses stays, Vrabel has to believe the veteran’s steadiness outweighs the limits this protection has shown.

And I understand the fans who want the change right now. Watching a first-round pick sit on the sideline while Maye takes pressure is frustrating.

But playing a rookie just for the sake of playing him isn’t development. Lomu has to handle speed off the edge, counter moves inside, and adjustments before the snap. And the coaches have to compare the run blocking too, not just pass protection.

So demand a real competition. Absolutely. Just don’t promise that switching starters fixes the whole line.

And remember what Vrabel has said about Lomu before. He’s 21. He’s new to the league. He’s learning a hard position. But he’s improved. That’s a coach who sees the progress, but isn’t going to rush it.

And there’s another reason this decision carries so much weight. Maye’s protection depends on the players around the tackle.

If you keep someone in to help block, the offense loses a target running a route. If you send everybody out, the tackle has to handle more on his own. So the right tackle decision actually changes the shape of the entire passing game.

Watch the practices this week. Watch the lineup in Munich. And watch the first pressured third downs. Those will show whether Vrabel sees Lomu as ready now, or still developing.

And with the receivers and running backs this banged up, every available route matters more than ever.

Because look at what this offense looks like right now. According to NESN, the Patriots head into the Lions game with Cory Ker leading the backfield, and DeMario Douglas as their top available wide receiver.

Think about how different that is from the plan before the season. The idea was Rhamondre Stevenson and TreVeyon Henderson sharing the running game. And A.J. Brown leading a receiver group with Romeo Doubs, Hunter Henry, and Douglas.

Now, Josh McDaniels doesn’t just have to replace names. He has to redraw responsibilities.

Let’s go through it.

Rhamondre Stevenson is expected to miss a short stretch with the knee injury he suffered against Green Bay.

TreVeyon Henderson remains away from the team, after describing a medical emergency involving neurological symptoms with no public explanation. His health comes first, before any guess about a return date. And we’re all wishing him a full recovery.

So it’s Cory Ker’s backfield now. And he showed something against the Packers, with 55 yards on just eight carries. That’s almost seven yards a carry. Remember, he’s the guy who carried the ball eight straight times to close out the Jets game. Vrabel has praised his energy and his work ethic.

But eight carries don’t show us how Ker responds when a defense plans specifically for him. Or how much pass protection he can reliably handle. That’s the next test.

And Lan Larison had just three carries over the previous two games. So the depth behind Ker is still very thin.

Smash that like button if you believe in Cory Ker.

At receiver, Romeo Doubs is out with a shoulder injury, and he’s hoping to return after the Week 11 bye. A.J. Brown has missed two straight games with a knee problem, and he was considered unlikely for this week.

So the healthy receivers are DeMario Douglas, Mack Hollins, Efton Chism III, and Kyle Williams. Plus Hunter Henry at tight end, if his foot holds up, and rookie Eli Raridon.

And the injuries don’t stop at the skill positions. Mike Onwenu has been on injured reserve since September. Guards Alijah Vera-Tucker and Greg Van Roten are out too. So the interior of the line is still patched together.

And here’s the problem that creates. A patched-together line might need a running back or tight end to stay in and help block. And if Ker is still learning the protection calls, the staff might need to simplify the reads, or keep another blocker in. Either way, that takes options away from Maye, when two of his most important targets are already out.

But a good coaching staff can’t wait for a perfect roster. And there are real things to work with.

Douglas is already producing. Ker has shown burst. Hunter Henry is still an option in the passing game. Mack Hollins just came back from injured reserve and caught a 22-yard touchdown in his first game back. McDaniels can vary formations, use motion before the snap, and lean on quick throws.

But motion doesn’t block a defender who comes free because nobody picked him up. And quick throws don’t replace a real deep threat. The answer has to show up in plays the available players can actually execute.

So here’s my read. This isn’t some romantic chance for the backups. It’s a test of trust.

Ker finds out whether his runs can turn into a weekly responsibility. Douglas has to carry more volume, when opponents know he’s one of Maye’s clearest paths to the ball. And Drake Maye shouldn’t be asked to make up for every missing piece by himself.

Remember what Maye said after the Packers game. He took the blame for all three interceptions. He said he has to start games better, see the field better, and play from ahead. He’s holding himself accountable. But Vrabel and McDaniels have to give him a plan that works. They have to define who protects, who gets out into routes, and which plays still work once Detroit recognizes the formation.

Now, if the offense is feeling the cost of the missing pieces, the defense can’t afford to lose the ones it still has. And that brings us to Christian Barmore.

Barmore stayed in New England after the deadline. Even though his name came up constantly in trade talk. Albert Breer had reported the Patriots were open to moving him. A national report said he and Carlton Davis were available.

And now? He’s still a Patriot heading into Week 10.

Musket Fire called him one of the winners of the decision to keep the roster together. And the article described Barmore as having one of his best seasons.

And remember what Mike Vrabel said when he was asked about those trade reports last week. He said he hadn’t shared that information with anybody. He said national reports have a tendency to drum things up this time of year. And instead of talking about trades, he talked about coaching Barmore on clearing his feet on his pass rush, after Barmore got a sack in Miami.

Now, let’s be careful. Trade rumors don’t show us what was actually said between teams. A name circulating and a player being pushed out are two very different things. Sometimes other teams ask about a player even when his own team has no desire to sell.

But I’d have a hard time understanding a trade that weakened the interior of this defensive line right now. This defense just gave up 45 points.

And Barmore matters. An inside pass rusher who forces blockers to slide toward the middle makes life easier for the players rushing off the edge. Elijah Ponder. Gabe Jacas. Barmore shrinks the quarterback’s space and helps the rotation keep pressure on the pocket. He doesn’t need a sack on every play for his role to matter.

And his numbers back it up. Even in reduced snaps this season, he’s had 12 pressures, three and a half sacks, a 9.3 percent pressure rate, and a pass rush grade close to 80.

Smash that like button if you’re glad Christian Barmore is staying in Foxborough.

Now, there’s a fair counterpoint. Keeping a productive player doesn’t automatically prove that keeping him was the best possible decision. If an offer had come in that addressed another big need, or improved how the roster is built long-term, Eliot Wolf would have had to listen. And moving Barmore could have freed up about 20 million dollars in future cap space.

But we haven’t seen any offer like that. So calling it an automatic win is a little too easy too.

What we can judge is the immediate result. Vrabel didn’t lose an important piece on the inside, right when he needs his defense to respond.

So against Detroit, watch Barmore. Does he stay on the field for the big downs? Does he draw double teams? Does he create room for his teammates to make plays? If this defense generates better pressure against Jared Goff, keeping Barmore will have a result we can actually see.

And on offense, another player who stayed gets a different kind of opening. DeMario Douglas.

Douglas still has the chance to become a central piece of this passing game. Because the trade for Josh Downs never happened.

Musket Fire linked the two, because they play similar roles. And the article suggested Downs would have cut into Douglas’s snaps. But that competition never became real.

What is real is Douglas heading into the Lions game with more responsibility than ever, especially with A.J. Brown and Romeo Doubs out.

But Patriots Nation, this is where things get really interesting.

A quiet deadline isn’t proof that the offense is complete. It just puts Douglas’s performance under a brighter light. The snaps and targets that might have gone to a new receiver are still available to the players in the building. And Douglas can use this opening to make the case for a bigger role, even after the starters come back.

And he’s earned it. According to NESN, Douglas leads the team with 434 receiving yards and four touchdowns. Remember, he recovered the onside kick that sealed the Jets win. He had seven catches for 86 yards and a touchdown in that game. And against the Packers, he was one of the few bright spots on offense.

He didn’t get this opportunity just because other players are hurt. He’s been producing all season.

But the strongest argument against treating Douglas as the complete answer is this. These receivers do different jobs. A player who works well in tight spaces doesn’t automatically replace everything A.J. Brown offers as the lead target, or everything Romeo Doubs adds outside.

If Detroit crowds the areas where Maye likes to find Douglas, other routes have to punish that choice. That tests the whole receiving group, and McDaniels’s play calls.

But if Douglas still produces against a defense that’s focused on him, then the return of injured players stops being a reason to automatically cut his snaps. The offense could pair him with the starters, instead of choosing between them. And with Brown and Doubs healthy, Douglas might even find more space, because of the attention those outside receivers draw.

That would give Maye a reliable option inside, without asking Douglas to do the same job as the receivers outside. Their recoveries, and their timing together, will decide whether that picture becomes real.

So watch the target distribution in Munich. Watch the kinds of routes Douglas runs. And watch whether Maye can keep the ball moving after Detroit makes its first defensive adjustment.

We still don’t know who starts on the right side, or exactly how this offense will function with fewer options. Practices this week, and the first possessions against Detroit, will show whether preparation turns into execution.

And after Munich, the bye week. A chance to rest, heal, and get some of these key players back for the stretch run.
