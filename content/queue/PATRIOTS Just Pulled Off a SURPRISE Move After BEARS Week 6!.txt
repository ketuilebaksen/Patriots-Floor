Let’s start with the play that had everyone talking. Rhamondre Stevenson.

Stevenson made New England’s most explosive play of the 30 to 24 loss in Chicago. He caught a pass before halftime and went 48 yards to the end zone.

And remember what Drake Maye said about that play after the game. He had a feeling the Bears were going to blitz. With five out in the pattern and Chicago bringing five, it left four blockers and Stevenson with the ball against six defenders. All it takes is one cut. And that’s exactly how they drew it up. They’d been practicing that play since OTAs.

Stevenson finished with five catches, 93 yards, and a touchdown. A career high in receiving yards.

But here’s the other number. The one that gets under my skin. Six carries for 22 yards.

As a receiver, Stevenson made Chicago pay. As a runner, he barely had room to get started.

And this wasn’t just a Stevenson problem. Take out Maye’s scrambles, and the Patriots ran for 44 yards on 13 carries. That’s 3.4 yards a carry.

And here’s what makes it worse. The Bears’ run defense was vulnerable by several measures coming into this game. This looked like a real chance to take some weight off the quarterback. And New England couldn’t turn it into production.

Mike Vrabel admitted it after the game. He said he expected them to run the ball better. And Drake Maye said the same thing. They have to get the ground game going.

When the head coach and the quarterback see the exact same weakness, one spectacular touchdown can’t erase it.

Now, I already know the impatient response. Just get Stevenson the ball. He solved it through the air.

But a catch and a handoff ask completely different things. On that long reception, Stevenson got the ball in space, with room to work. On a run, the blocks have to create a path before the defense closes it.

And look at who was blocking. Walter Rouse and Matt Prior played the whole night at guard. Rouse was making his first career start. Prior was on the practice squad just three weeks ago. That left the line relying on deep reserves, in a job that depends completely on communication.

That explains some of the squeeze. But it doesn’t excuse every failed run.

Look at the rest of the backfield. TreVeyon Henderson picked up nine yards on three carries. Cory Ker got 13 yards on four. And the backs combined for negative expected points added per carry.

Now, that doesn’t mean they all share one technical flaw. It means the calls, the blocks, and the decisions kept leaving the offense in longer downs. And longer downs make the passes predictable, at a time when the line was already pieced together.

To be fair to the staff, there’s a real defense here. Chicago loaded the box at times and basically invited New England to throw. And Maye did. He threw for 321 yards and two touchdowns. And Stevenson became a weapon in space. Running straight into a stacked front would have been stubborn.

But the Patriots still need to gain yards on the ground when the defense isn’t offering an easy pass. A real offense chooses how it hurts an opponent. It can’t let the opponent decide every series.

So next game, I’m watching the early runs, and the guards, before I judge anybody in the backfield. Did the runner find the cut? Did the play gain useful yards? Or did it leave Maye with another miserable third down?

Because if the run game stays stuck, the calls for outside help are going to get louder. Fan pressure is one thing. A real trade opening is another.

And that brings us to the surprise move being discussed. Jonathan Taylor.

His name came up because New England’s running game needs help. One analysis suggested the Patriots should check in with the Colts after their front office change. Remember, Indianapolis just fired general manager Chris Ballard. And we’ve already seen Quenton Nelson’s name pop up as a possible trade target from that same situation.

The fit is worth talking about. But let’s be clear. A door that might open isn’t the same as Jonathan Taylor sitting by the phone waiting for Foxborough to call.

Taylor is central to the Colts’ offense. He recently signed an extension. And he’d be expensive to acquire.

So the real question is this. What problem would Taylor solve that this roster can’t solve on its own?

But Patriots Nation, this is where things get really interesting.

If the staff believes this line can still open lanes, Taylor would raise the ceiling on the ground and force defenses to respect the backfield. He has proven production. He can contribute as a receiver. Pair him with Stevenson, and Maye gets two veteran options in the backfield, without waiting for Henderson to develop overnight.

Imagine it. A defense loads the box against Taylor, and Maye throws over the top. A defense drops back to stop the pass, and Taylor runs through the middle. That’s the balance this offense has been missing.

I understand why fans want the front office to at least find out the price.

But the best argument against getting carried away is staring us right in the face. Taylor can’t make a guard block better just by showing up. If contact comes early in the backfield, that big name becomes a very expensive purchase of hard-fought yards.

Those 44 yards on 13 designed runs in Chicago reflect the whole system. So before spending premium picks, or giving up on Henderson, I want to know what the coaches are getting from the line, the calls, and the backs. Without a real diagnosis, the headline changes while the problem stays the same.

And let’s talk about Henderson, because his usage stood out.

Henderson played only 13 offensive snaps against the Bears. Three runs for nine yards, and two catches for 14. Ker saw six snaps.

That’s a small role for a player who was expected to add burst to this offense. But it doesn’t prove Josh McDaniels has given up on him.

Vrabel actually explained it after the game. He said they had certain packages and personnel groupings for Henderson, and that he’ll help them. But they also wanted to see where they were with Cory Ker getting some carries. And he said Henderson helped them plenty down the stretch.

So questions about Henderson’s reads, broken tackles, and pass protection deserve real study. But not a career verdict after three carries. The staff might trust Stevenson more. Or it might be reacting to protection needs with backup guards in the lineup. We can see who played and how little Henderson was used. We don’t know the full reason yet.

Then comes the roster bill. Taylor is under contract, valuable, and important to Indianapolis. One column imagined a second-round pick, another mid-round pick, and a player going to the Colts. But that’s the writer’s proposal. It’s not the Colts’ price.

And even if it worked, losing those picks, and maybe a young back, would limit help elsewhere. The Patriots have already invested big in A.J. Brown. A decision this big has to account for the entire roster.

So picture the choice, if a trade became possible. Spend two picks to improve the backfield, while the line keeps losing starters? Or save those picks for protection and depth?

Remember, there are cheaper offensive line options out there. ESPN’s Bill Barnwell suggested a trade for Andrew Wylie for just a late-round pick swap. And Quenton Nelson is still the dream on the line. Every pick spent on a running back is a pick not spent on protecting Drake Maye.

I wouldn’t rule out the Taylor route. At a manageable cost, he could immediately draw more defensive attention to New England’s runs. But I won’t throw Henderson into a deal just because he spent most of one night on the sideline. If he can still earn a role, trading him now gives up that chance before better conditions can test him.

So fans demanding action are right. Maye shouldn’t have to carry every offensive flaw on his own. If the front office sees a fit, checking on Taylor’s availability makes sense. But paying a star’s price before understanding the line and Henderson could be premature.

The next public sign would be credible reporting of interest between the teams, and a possible cost. Until then, watch how New England divides the carries and snaps, and whether better blocking makes any back more dangerous.

And Chicago showed us another answer that’s already inside this offense.

Hunter Henry.

Henry caught all seven passes Drake Maye threw his way. Seven for seven. He finished with 71 yards in the loss.

And none of those catches went for more than 16 yards. So one big play didn’t inflate his total. On a night when the offense leaned on explosive moments and kept chasing the score, that steady production was something real it could build on.

Remember what Vrabel said about Henry before this game. A consistent presence. A veteran leader. He’s performed and produced every year of his career. And quarterbacks like guys who are dependable. Henry proved it again.

Let’s look at the whole receiving picture. Maye threw for 321 yards and two touchdowns, but he also threw that interception in the end zone. Henry led the team in catches. Stevenson had more receiving yards, mostly because of the long touchdown. Eli Raridon scored and gained 56 yards on three catches. A.J. Brown caught three of six targets. And DeMario Douglas had three catches for 23 yards.

Henry provided the reliable connection between the plays that grabbed all the attention. Every target to him turned into a completion. And the offense needed every one of them.

If you love Hunter Henry’s reliability, smash that like button right now.

But seven catches aren’t a complete answer. So why didn’t the Patriots win, if he was that dependable?

Because one reliable target can’t erase an interception, drives that don’t finish, or a run game stuck at 44 yards. The late field goal still left New England needing an onside kick. And it didn’t recover.

Henry handled his part of the job. The rest of the offense needed to turn that support into points earlier, while there was still time to change who controlled the game.

That’s where his consistency has to become part of a sequence, not just a nice stat line. One 10-yard catch can keep a defense on the field and set up the next call. But another 10-yard catch after a run that went nowhere might only repair the damage.

Chicago forced New England into that second situation way too often. Henry helped win back ground. But the offense should be creating manageable downs before asking Maye for another tough conversion.

And those seven completions point to a tactical choice too. With backup guards playing, asking a tight end to help block might be necessary. But when Henry is tied up in protection, Maye loses a route he clearly trusts.

If the line can hold up, McDaniels can release Henry into the middle of the field earlier. If it can’t, he has to choose between keeping the quarterback protected and keeping that target available.

That perfect catch line tells us exactly what the offense gives up when Henry gets taken out of the passing design. And it doesn’t make the protection question go away, even with the extra time before Miami.

So that’s the adjustment I’m watching. On a key third down, is Henry out in a route, or staying in to save the pocket? And when he does release, can Maye find him before the play turns into improvisation?

Now let’s talk about the defense. Because one absence is pushing a proven player into a job that suits him a lot less.

Carlton Davis III missed his third straight game with a neck injury. And the Patriots secondary felt that hole against Chicago.

Christian Gonzalez is still holding down one side. But on the other side, thin depth has pushed Marcus Jones outside more often than New England would want.

Jones has already proven how valuable he can be in the slot. But asking him to match that level on the boundary, against different-sized targets and different angles, asks one strength to cover two different jobs.

I don’t mind asking a good player to adapt. I do mind when the emergency adjustment starts to look like the whole plan.

Look at the numbers credited to Pro Football Focus. Jones has already played 93 snaps outside this season, compared with 60 in all of last season. His coverage grades against the Raiders and the Bears were 48.8 and 37.4. And he was credited with six missed tackles in Chicago.

Six. And remember what Vrabel said after the game. The tackling wasn’t good enough. Not against this team. Whether it’s D’Andre Swift, Luther Burden, or Caleb Williams.

Now, one grade can’t describe every coverage snap. And a missed tackle also depends on positioning, on help, and on the job the defense assigned. But that massive change in role, alongside those recorded struggles, raises a fair question about fit. Is this team putting Jones where he helps most? Or just where it needs a body?

And the matchup with Caleb Williams makes any easy diagnosis harder. Keeping more defenders facing a mobile quarterback and playing zone coverage can be a smart answer to scrambling plays. The analysis says New England has blitzed less since the Buffalo game.

According to reporter Evan Lazar, Williams went 7 of 10 against zone coverage for 117 yards. The defense tried to keep its eyes on the quarterback, and it gave receivers space in the first half.

You can’t charge every one of those yards to Davis being out. But you can say his absence limited the combinations the defense could use. And Chicago took advantage of the space it found.

There’s a fair counterargument. Getting Davis back doesn’t force Vrabel to play man coverage on every down. And it doesn’t erase missed tackles. Against another mobile quarterback, the staff might still choose zone as a strategy.

I agree. The difference is having that choice, without moving Jones out of position because there’s no better option.

With Davis opposite Gonzalez, Jones would have a path back to more slot snaps, where his traits have historically played better. And the defense could have more freedom to mix coverages and pick its pressure spots, if the rest of the unit executes. That’s a tactical possibility. Not a magic switch.

So tell me in the comments, Patriots Nation. If Davis returns, what’s the first change you’d need to see before you trust this secondary again? Give me something you can actually spot. Jones back in the slot? Fewer easy catches against zone? Tackles finished in space?

And here’s the good news. Davis was a limited practice participant before he was ruled out for Chicago. And the longer break before Miami gives him a real chance to return. But the participation report and the game designation will tell us more than hope will.

Meanwhile, the defense has urgent work to do on the plays that would still be bad even with its starter back. The tackling. The space against zone. The fundamentals.

So let’s put it all together.

The surprise move on the table: Jonathan Taylor, a proven back from a Colts team in transition. A possible fit, but an expensive one, and no credible reports of talks yet.

The problem it would solve: a run game that produced 44 yards on 13 designed carries, even against a vulnerable Bears run defense.

The answer already in the building: Hunter Henry, seven catches on seven targets, and Rhamondre Stevenson, with a career-high 93 receiving yards and a 48-yard touchdown.

And the hole on defense: Carlton Davis missing his third straight game, pushing Marcus Jones outside, where he struggled with coverage and six missed tackles.

Chicago exposed a team that can make difficult plays, but still can’t make the basics reliable for four quarters. The Foxborough standard demands better execution, and roster decisions that fit the actual problem.

A running back trade is still an open question. So is Davis’s return. And I want to see the offensive line open space in the early series against Miami, and the secondary adjust its roles once the active list comes out.
