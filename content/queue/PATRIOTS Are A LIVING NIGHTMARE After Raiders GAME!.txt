Let’s start with Jeanty, because that’s the headline everybody’s chasing. Ashton Jeanty finished the week questionable with ankle and foot problems, and his practice week tells the whole story. Full participation on Wednesday. Limited on Thursday. No practice at all on Friday. That’s a player trending in the wrong direction as the week went on.

Now let me be careful here, because I’m not going to sell you a fake narrative. That practice pattern does not tell us he’ll miss the game against the Patriots. Players sit out Friday all the time and still suit up on Sunday. What it does tell us is that Las Vegas has to prepare another option for a guy who’s been a big part of both their running game and their passing game. That matters, because when a team builds its weekly plan around a player and then has to rebuild it around somebody else, things get messy. Timing changes. Roles change. Comfort changes.

Let’s talk numbers. Through four games, Jeanty has 78 carries for 264 yards, and he’s added 19 catches for 165 yards. That’s a lot of involvement. That’s enough touches that if he can’t go, the Raiders’ entire game plan has to shift.

But read those numbers carefully. The rushing yardage doesn’t point to some unstoppable ground attack. 264 yards on 78 carries isn’t a guy gashing defenses every week. So if you’re worried about Jeanty running all over the Patriots, the numbers say slow down a bit.

The receiving side is where he really matters. Jeanty is a real option out of the backfield. When he releases into a route, a linebacker has to respect him. The coverage has to communicate. And if they don’t, a short little dump-off turns into a first down, and suddenly a drive that should’ve stalled keeps going. That’s the hidden value of a back like that. It’s not always the 60-yard run. Sometimes it’s the 7-yard catch on third-and-5 that keeps the chains moving and the defense on the field.

If Jeanty sits, rookie Mike Washington Jr. could take on more of that work. And here’s the part people miss. That changes the Raiders’ workload, but it doesn’t make them predictable. A new back means new tendencies, and New England’s defense hasn’t seen as much of them. So no, one questionable player does not decide this matchup. Not even close.

The real reason nobody should be celebrating early is sitting on New England’s own injury report. And Patriots Nation, this is the part that should have your full attention.

Christian Gonzalez. Out. Carlton Davis III. Out. Channing Canada. Out. Karan Prunty. Out.

Two starters and two depth options, all at the same position. That’s not a small issue. That’s a whole cornerback room getting ripped apart in one week. And a coach can’t hide that with one substitution. You can’t just plug one guy in and pretend nothing changed.

Think about how coverage works. You can send help to the outside. You can roll a safety over the top. You can bracket a receiver. But every time you send help somewhere, somebody has to leave another area. Football is a game of trade-offs. You protect against the deep ball, and maybe you cut down that big-play risk, but now you’ve opened a shorter window underneath. And who’s on the other side waiting to throw into those windows? Kirk Cousins. A veteran quarterback who has spent his whole career finding exactly those kinds of soft spots.

Now, to be fair, the Raiders have their own problems. Las Vegas will be without Jalen Nailor, Dont’e Thornton, and Trey Zoo. That’s real. That’s receiving depth they’re missing too.

And Cousins can still punish late communication in the secondary. That’s the danger with backup corners. It’s not always that they’re slower or less talented. It’s that they haven’t played as many snaps together. They haven’t built that instinct of “I pass this guy to you, you pick him up.” When that handoff comes half a second late, a veteran quarterback sees it and throws it.

And if Jeanty does play, even at less than full strength, ignoring him as a receiver could hand the Raiders manageable third downs all afternoon. You can’t build the whole plan around Bowers and forget the back. That’s how you lose a game without ever giving up a big play.

The other side of the ball matters too, and this is where the game could really be won or lost.

The Raiders have forced nine turnovers through four games. That’s the most in the league so far. Nine. This is a defense that doesn’t just try to stop you. It tries to take the ball out of your hands. Strips, tipped passes, jumped routes. They’re hunting for it.

Now the good news. Drake Maye practiced fully despite the right shoulder issue on the report, and he received no game designation. Good. That’s exactly what you want to hear about your franchise quarterback heading into a game like this.

But even a fully healthy Maye is still facing a defense that attacks the football. And that’s the whole key to this game for me. New England cannot hand Cousins short fields and extra possessions. Think about how dangerous that combination is. You’ve got a thin secondary on one side, and a turnover-hungry defense on the other. Every giveaway doesn’t just cost you a drive. It puts your patched-up corners right back on the field, on a short field, against a quarterback who knows how to finish.

And then there’s Maxx Crosby. If Raiders pressure makes every downfield throw feel desperate, Crosby gets more and more chances to come after Maye. That’s the nightmare scenario for the Patriots. Long down-and-distance, Maye holding the ball, Crosby screaming off the edge, and the ball coming loose. That’s how a team with nine takeaways turns into a team with eleven.

So what’s the first thing to watch? The active list. Does Jeanty play? And what combination of defenders can New England actually put on the field against Bowers? Only once we know those two answers can we measure how much of an advantage that possible absence really is. Until then, it’s all speculation, and I’m not here to sell you speculation as fact.

But let’s be real about what Sunday demands. A running back needs acceleration. He needs to plant and cut. He needs to take repeated contact, snap after snap. A knee that got rolled up on a week ago has to handle all of that. So the lineup will give us confirmation, sure. But his workload is what’s going to tell us the real story. Does he get 8 touches or 18? Does he play on third down? Does he come out after every big hit? That’s where the truth is.

And here’s why it matters so much. If Stevenson can share the work with TreVeyon Henderson, Josh McDaniels has options against a clear weakness. Opponents have averaged 134.5 rushing yards per game against Las Vegas. That’s one of the worst marks in the entire league. There’s an opening there. A real one.

But listen to me, Patriots Nation. That is not a blank check to just run the ball every snap. The Raiders have Maxx Crosby. They can bring pressure. They know how to take the ball away. If McDaniels keeps calling runs that go nowhere, what happens? Second-and-9. Third-and-8. And now Maye is in exactly the situation we just talked about, holding the ball against a defense built to punish him.

So I don’t want an empty total of rushing attempts. I don’t care if they run it 35 times if half of those go for one yard. I want gains that change the next call. A 5-yard run on first down that makes second down manageable. A run that pulls the safeties down so the play-action shot opens up. That’s what good running looks like. That’s what this offense needs.

Christian Barmore is questionable too. Yet he practiced fully on Friday after two limited days. That’s the opposite of Jeanty’s week. Jeanty went full, limited, nothing. Barmore went limited, limited, full. That’s a player trending in the right direction at the right time.

And his possible return changes the other side of this whole equation. With four corners ruled out, it’s tempting to demand that a patched-up secondary win every single route on its own. That’s not realistic. That’s not fair to those players. But interior pressure? That could cut down the time Cousins has to find those matchups in the first place.

Think about it this way. A backup corner covering a receiver for four seconds is in big trouble. That same backup corner covering for two seconds, because the pocket is collapsing in Cousins’ face, has a fighting chance. Barmore can’t erase a blown coverage. Nobody can. And his game status is still open. But asking a backup to cover indefinitely is very different from asking him to hold up until the front disrupts the play.

That’s how you protect a thin secondary. You don’t just protect it from behind. You protect it from up front.

Now, there’s a counterpoint, and I want to be fair about it. Without Jeanty, Las Vegas might lean into quick throws to take the pressure out of the game. Three-step drops, ball out fast, get it to Bowers on a short route and let him work. That blunts a pass rush. It’s a smart counter.

So Barmore isn’t a one-man rescue plan. Let’s not pretend he is. What he could do is squeeze the pocket and give New England more freedom to choose when to blitz. That’s huge. If the Patriots can get pressure with their front four without pulling extra defenders out of coverage, those missing corners become a little less exposed. Every guy you don’t have to send on a blitz is one more guy helping in the back end.

But if they can’t get home with the front? Then Cousins has time. And with time, he’ll find the space those absences create. That’s the matchup. That’s the battle. Not a promise of what Barmore will do, but the key to whether this defense holds up.

Against Maxx Crosby and a team leading the league in takeaways, those small wins stop feeling small. Every 4-yard run on first down is one less obvious passing situation. Every clean blitz pickup is one less shot Crosby gets at Maye’s blind side. They cut down on the moments when Maye has to hold the ball and invent a tough conversion under pressure. That’s how you beat a turnover-hungry defense. You don’t give it the moments it feeds on.

Stevenson himself talked about staying on the field, keeping the defense fresh, and avoiding long downs. And with the secondary this thin, that’s not just an offensive goal. That’s a defensive strategy. Every sustained drive means fewer immediate chances for Cousins to test those reserves. Every eight-minute drive is eight minutes your backup corners are standing on the sideline instead of covering Bowers.

But, and this is a big but, burning clock means very little if the drive ends with a fumble. A long drive that ends in a turnover is worse than a quick three-and-out, because now you’re tired on offense, your defense is cold, and Cousins has the ball in great field position. New England needs efficiency, not just a slower pace.

If Las Vegas shuts down the interior, Henderson could offer a different answer. More burst to the outside, a different look. But the calls still have to fit the protection that’s working. You can’t call a perimeter run behind a blocking unit that’s getting beat on the edge.
