Let’s start with what Vrabel said. Because it set the tone for this whole week.

Vrabel told WEEI that Detroit beat the Patriots in all four phases. And he said his team has to willingly participate and execute.

That’s hard to hear after a 35 to 10 loss. And even harder with Drake Maye injured, and the team heading into its bye week at 5 and 5.

Now, a lot of fans want to point at players who supposedly quit. And I understand the anger. But Vrabel’s full answer deserves a careful reading.

Vrabel admitted that Detroit was better coached, and that the Lions played harder. But when he was asked whether his message had stopped getting through to his players, he didn’t say the players had stopped believing in him.

He talked about execution. He said what looked sharp in preparation didn’t show up consistently in the game. And he made it clear that everybody owns a piece of that. Players and coaches.

So here’s where I stand. If I turned that interview into a made-up list of players who don’t care, I’d be letting the coaching staff, the game plan, and the adjustments off the hook. And I’m not doing that.

But I’m also not going to soften the demand. Because there’s a standard here.

When your starting quarterback goes down, some players might feel the game slipping away. And that’s exactly when you find out whether a team’s habits are real.

A receiver has to finish his route, even if the ball might not be coming his way. A lineman has to hold his block on the backside of the play. And a defender who loses the first step has to keep chasing the ball carrier, because getting there in time can turn a touchdown into a short gain.

Now, I’m not saying every mistake in Munich was a lack of effort. Technique matters. And so does the talent across from you. The Lions are a really good team. But the collective answer has to show up on the next snap. Not just in a Monday interview.

And there’s a fair counterpoint too. Losing Maye on the first series, and then facing that Detroit offense, can make any roster look slow, especially once the Lions take control of the game. Context matters.

That’s why I want to look at the snaps when New England still had a chance to respond, and the drives right after halftime. Was the communication there before the snap? Did everybody pursue the ball? Did the offense run its routes and make its blocks, even when the pass fell incomplete?

That’s how accountability becomes a real football evaluation, instead of a judgment about someone’s character.

Now here’s where it might get uncomfortable.

If the coaching staff finds repeated failures of effort or assignment on film, it has the bye week to change who gets the reps, and to cut back the role of anyone who keeps falling short. Salary, name, and resume shouldn’t buy a player another unfinished play.

But changing the lineup without teaching the responsibilities better would just be theater. It has to be both.

So here’s what I want to see. The first practice after the bye. How the snaps get distributed. And how this team reacts to its first mistake against the Chargers. There’s a real chance to compete, even with a backup quarterback. But the whole team has to play like every down belongs to them too.

Now let’s get to the surprise move. The quarterback decision.

MassLive reporter Mark Daniels says the Patriots plan to start Tommy DeVito, and have rookie Behren Morton run the scout team, while Drake Maye is out.

And let’s be careful here. Daniels attributes that to a league source. It’s the current plan. It’s not an official announcement of the lineup for the rest of the season. But it answers the most urgent question after Vrabel’s challenge. Who gets the reps to get this offense back on its feet?

And the answer is Tommy DeVito.

Let’s look at what DeVito did in Munich. He came in against Detroit and completed 12 of 24 passes for 81 yards, with one touchdown and one interception. The offense barely moved.

But it would be unfair to define him by an emergency appearance in a game that was already getting away from New England. Coming in cold in the first quarter is very different from preparing all week as the starter.

A full week as the starter gives him time to rehearse the calls and the adjustments for a specific opponent. So if he starts against the Chargers, that game will tell us a lot more about him than Munich did.

And that confidence in DeVito didn’t come out of nowhere.

This offseason, the Patriots signed DeVito to a two-year deal, and they released Joshua Dobbs. That was a clear choice. And according to Daniels, the team viewed DeVito as a solid backup, and it likes the way rookie Behren Morton has been developing.

DeVito has started games in the NFL before. That explains why the team trusts him. But it doesn’t convert third downs. What really matters is whether he sees the pressure coming, and gets the ball out on time.

And here’s where things get really interesting. Choosing DeVito also tests the coaching staff.

If the game plan still calls for plays that depended on Drake Maye’s mobility, his aggressiveness, and his ability to improvise, then the coaches will be measuring the backup against the wrong standard. DeVito isn’t Maye. And the offense can’t pretend he is.

So the protection has to give him a way out when pressure comes. The receivers have to be open when he reaches them in his progression. And the running game has to take some of the weight off his shoulders.

That doesn’t mean calling only short, predictable throws. It means building drives where an efficient first down creates options on the next play, instead of needing a miracle every time third down shows up.

Now, what about Jimmy Garoppolo?

Some fans would rather bring in Garoppolo because of his connection to Josh McDaniels from their years in New England and in Las Vegas. And I get it. Experience can be reassuring when your starter goes down.

But Daniels raised a fair counterpoint. DeVito already knows this roster and this offense. And there’s no guarantee Garoppolo would arrive ready to play, or that he’d stay healthy.

So ask yourself. What would signing Garoppolo actually fix by the next game? A familiar name can’t improve the pass protection. It can’t bring injured receivers back by itself.

Now, if Maye’s absence drags on, and the current quarterback room can’t hold up in practice, then looking at the market becomes a different conversation. But right now, the team is going with what it has.

And Morton keeps developing while he runs the scout team. That doesn’t prove he’s ready to lead the offense on Sunday. But if DeVito struggles, the staff can compare how the adjusted game plan is working, how far the rookie has come, and what outside options exist after those first practices.

So here’s my demand of the coaching staff. Give DeVito a clear game plan, and hold the entire roster accountable for executing it.

If the opening drive against the Chargers has organized protection, open outlets, and play calls that fit DeVito, then the staff did its job. But if pass rushers come in free and the receivers are out of sync, changing quarterbacks won’t solve anything.

And getting a receiver back could open up a lot more options.

Romeo Doubs is eligible to come off injured reserve after the bye. And the report expects that he could play against the Chargers. His injury was to his shoulder, after he landed on it on a touchdown catch against the Jets.

But “could” is the key word. We still need an official activation, and we need to see him practice. I’m not treating his return as a done deal.

Still, for an offense that may start DeVito, and that just watched A.J. Brown leave the Detroit game with a hamstring injury, getting a reliable target back matters a lot.

And look at what Doubs did before he got hurt. In six games this season, he had 14 catches for 261 yards and three touchdowns, according to the team’s own review. That production explains why his absence squeezed the whole receiver rotation.

Now, here’s an important point about the receivers. They’re not interchangeable.

DeMario Douglas brings speed from the slot. He’s been the team’s most productive receiver this season, leading the team with 434 receiving yards and four touchdowns. His work inside can open up room across the field.

Mack Hollins brings a more physical presence on the outside. He came back from IR with a touchdown against the Packers.

And Doubs can give DeVito a clear option in his progression, especially if defenses crowd the short middle of the field.

Those are three different traits. Having three names available doesn’t mean you have three players doing the same job. And that distinction matters even more with a backup quarterback. You can’t just hand out A.J. Brown’s targets to whoever’s left. It takes practice, coordinated routes, proper spacing, and timing.

And a receiver coming back from injury might have his snaps managed too. The staff has to plan for that.

Now, I know the objection from fans who are tired of waiting. A promised return doesn’t win a game. And Doubs was inconsistent in his small sample before the injury. Fantasy numbers don’t fix pass protection or quarterback reads.

That’s fair. But this is still encouraging, because it could give the offense another tool. If Brown stays out, defenses can keep testing New England on the outside. But if Doubs plays a full role, and builds timing with DeVito, the defense will have to respect more than one route on the big downs.

We need to see it happen on the field first.

So here are the signals I’m watching, before Doubs even makes his first catch. Does he get activated? Can he practice without a real limitation? Where does he line up in the opening packages?

And then I want to see whether Douglas and Hollins stay in roles that make sense for them, instead of just inheriting extra targets because somebody else is hurt.
